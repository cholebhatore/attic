A service averages 80 ms per request, yet one user in a hundred waits over 2 seconds, and pages that call it 20 times are slow for most users. A database at 70 percent CPU is fine; at 90 percent, latency triples although traffic only grew by a quarter. Rewriting a function to be twice as fast speeds up the request by 3 percent. Each of these surprises is predicted by a small set of models. Knowing them turns optimization from guessing into arithmetic.

## The plain-language version

**Where this sits:** the first page of Optimization; the frontend and backend pages apply these models.

Most performance behaviour follows from a few facts: the slowest part of a sequence dominates it; work queues up nonlinearly as a system gets busy; and the rare slow cases, not the average, determine what most users experience when many requests combine.

## Latency, throughput, and utilisation

Latency and throughput are related but different. A single checkout lane serving a customer every 2 minutes has latency 2 minutes and throughput 30 per hour; adding a second lane doubles throughput but leaves each customer's service time at 2 minutes. Conversely, batching improves throughput while increasing latency for individual items.

| Change                            | Latency                                  | Throughput                  |
| :-------------------------------- | :--------------------------------------- | :-------------------------- |
| Add replicas (horizontal scaling) | Unchanged (or lower if queues were long) | Higher                      |
| Make each operation faster        | Lower                                    | Higher                      |
| Batch work                        | Higher per item                          | Higher                      |
| Cache results                     | Lower for hits                           | Higher                      |
| Add a queue in front of work      | Higher (waiting)                         | Smoother, protects capacity |

## Percentiles and why averages lie

Latency distributions are skewed: most requests are fast, a few are very slow (garbage collection, cache misses, lock waits, retries, slow disks).

Ten request latencies in ms: `20, 22, 21, 25, 23, 24, 22, 21, 23, 1900`.

| Statistic      | Value                                                                           |
| :------------- | :------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Mean           | 210 ms (describes no actual request)                                            |
| Median (p50)   | 22.5 ms                                                                         |
| p90            | ~25 ms by nearest rank (or ~212 ms with interpolation; small samples are noisy) |
| Max / p99 here | 1,900 ms                                                                        |

- The **mean** is pulled by outliers and hides both the typical case and the bad case.
- Report **p50** (typical), **p95/p99** (bad experiences that many users still hit), and **max** or **p99.9** for critical paths.
- **Percentiles cannot be averaged.** The average of per-server p99s is not the fleet's p99; aggregate raw data or histograms (Prometheus histograms, HDR histograms).
- **Coordinated omission**: load generators that wait for each response before sending the next under-report latency during stalls, because they stop sending exactly when the system is slow. Use open-loop load generators (constant arrival rate), such as k6's arrival-rate executors or wrk2.

## Tail latency and fan-out

When one user action depends on many requests, the slowest one decides the outcome.

If each backend call has a 1 percent chance of exceeding 1 second (its p99 is 1 s), the chance that a page making **N** independent calls hits at least one slow call is:

$$
P(\text{at least one slow}) = 1 - 0.99^{N}
$$

| Calls per page (N) | Chance the page sees a p99-slow call |
| :----------------- | :----------------------------------- |
| 1                  | 1%                                   |
| 10                 | ~10%                                 |
| 50                 | ~39%                                 |
| 100                | ~63%                                 |

A backend's p99 becomes the **typical** experience of a page that fans out to 100 calls. This is the central point of "The Tail at Scale" (Dean and Barroso, 2013).

Techniques for tail latency:

- Reduce fan-out: batch, aggregate on the server, cache.
- **Hedged requests**: if a request has not answered within, say, its p95, send a duplicate to another replica and use whichever answers first (a few percent of extra load for a large tail reduction). Only for idempotent reads.
- Timeouts with fallbacks for non-critical parts ([Resilience Patterns](/fullstack/building-for-scale/resilience-patterns)).
- Remove sources of variance: GC pauses, noisy neighbours, cold caches, lock contention, retries.

## Amdahl's law

If a fraction **p** of total time is sped up by a factor **s**, the overall speedup is:

$$
S = \frac{1}{(1 - p) + \frac{p}{s}}
$$

Worked example: an API request takes 400 ms: 300 ms waiting on the database, 60 ms of serialisation and business logic, 40 ms of network.

| Optimization                         | p    | s   | Overall speedup | New time |
| :----------------------------------- | :--- | :-- | :-------------- | :------- |
| Make business logic 2x faster        | 0.15 | 2   | 1.08x           | ~370 ms  |
| Make business logic infinitely fast  | 0.15 | ∞   | 1.18x           | 340 ms   |
| Make database part 3x faster (index) | 0.75 | 3   | 2.0x            | 200 ms   |

The ceiling for any optimization is `1 / (1 − p)`: shaving a step that is 15 percent of the time can never yield more than 1.18x. Always profile to find the largest **p** first.

The same law limits parallelism: if 10 percent of a job is inherently sequential, no number of cores makes it more than 10x faster.

## Little's law

For any stable system, the average number of items in the system **L** equals the arrival rate **λ** multiplied by the average time each spends in it **W**:

$$
L = \lambda \times W
$$

It holds regardless of distributions, which makes it useful for capacity planning:

| Question                                                                             | Calculation                                                         |
| :----------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | :------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| An API receives 500 req/s and requests take 200 ms. How many are in flight?          | L = 500 × 0.2 = **100 concurrent requests**                         |
| Each Node process handles ~50 concurrent DB-bound requests well. How many processes? | 100 / 50 = **2**, plus headroom                                     |
| A DB pool has 20 connections and queries take 10 ms. Max query throughput?           | λ = L / W = 20 / 0.01 = **2,000 queries/s**                         |
| Latency doubles to 400 ms under load (same traffic). Concurrency?                    | L = 500 × 0.4 = **200**: twice the connections, memory, and threads |

The last row explains cascading failures: when a dependency slows down, in-flight requests pile up in every caller, exhausting pools and memory even though traffic did not change. Timeouts cap **W**, which caps **L**.

## Queueing and the utilisation curve

A resource that is busy a fraction **ρ** of the time (utilisation) makes arriving work wait. For the simplest queueing model (M/M/1: random arrivals, random service times, one server), the average time in the system is:

$$
W = \frac{S}{1 - \rho}
$$

where **S** is the service time. With S = 10 ms:

| Utilisation ρ | Average time W | Relative to idle |
| :------------ | :------------- | :--------------- |
| 10%           | 11 ms          | 1.1x             |
| 50%           | 20 ms          | 2x               |
| 70%           | 33 ms          | 3.3x             |
| 80%           | 50 ms          | 5x               |
| 90%           | 100 ms         | 10x              |
| 95%           | 200 ms         | 20x              |
| 99%           | 1,000 ms       | 100x             |

Consequences:

- Latency rises **nonlinearly**: going from 80 to 90 percent utilisation doubles latency. Systems should run with headroom; autoscaling targets of 50 to 70 percent CPU are common for latency-sensitive services.
- Variability makes it worse: real services with variable service times queue more than the formula suggests at the same utilisation.
- A small slowdown in service time at high utilisation causes a large latency jump, which is why a "slightly slower" deploy can look like an outage.
- Separate queues (pools) per workload prevent one class of slow work from inflating everyone's wait ([bulkheads](/fullstack/building-for-scale/resilience-patterns)).

## The cost of round trips

Much web latency is round trips, not computation:

| Round trip                                  | Typical cost |
| :------------------------------------------ | :----------- |
| Function call in process                    | ~nanoseconds |
| Redis on the same network                   | ~0.2–1 ms    |
| Database query (indexed) in the same region | ~1–5 ms      |
| Service-to-service call in a region         | ~1–10 ms     |
| Browser to nearby CDN                       | ~10–30 ms    |
| Browser to a server on another continent    | ~100–250 ms  |

N sequential round trips multiply this cost: 30 sequential 3 ms queries (an N+1 pattern) cost 90 ms before any processing. The highest-leverage optimizations usually remove round trips (batching, joins, caching, parallelising, moving computation closer to the data or the user) rather than speeding up code.

## The cost hierarchy of operations

Orders of magnitude, approximate, varying by hardware (adapted from the widely cited "latency numbers every programmer should know"):

| Operation                           | Time         |
| :---------------------------------- | :----------- |
| L1 cache reference                  | ~1 ns        |
| Branch mispredict                   | ~3 ns        |
| Main memory reference               | ~100 ns      |
| Compress 1 KB (fast compressor)     | ~2 µs        |
| Read 1 MB sequentially from memory  | ~10–50 µs    |
| SSD random read                     | ~16–100 µs   |
| Round trip within a data centre     | ~500 µs      |
| Read 1 MB sequentially from SSD     | ~200 µs–1 ms |
| Disk (HDD) seek                     | ~2–10 ms     |
| Round trip California ↔ Netherlands | ~150 ms      |

Network and disk dominate memory and CPU by orders of magnitude. JSON serialisation of a large payload, however, can cost milliseconds of CPU, and on the browser's main thread, any millisecond counts against a 16 ms frame.

## Budgets

A **performance budget** is a limit that fails the build or alerts when exceeded:

| Budget type    | Example                                               |
| :------------- | :---------------------------------------------------- |
| User metric    | p75 LCP ≤ 2.5 s, INP ≤ 200 ms, CLS ≤ 0.1 (field data) |
| Lab metric     | Lighthouse performance score ≥ 90 on a reference page |
| Resource size  | Initial JS ≤ 170 KB compressed per route; CSS ≤ 50 KB |
| Request count  | ≤ 50 requests on the landing page                     |
| Server latency | p99 API latency ≤ 300 ms at target load               |
| Query count    | ≤ 10 database queries per request                     |

Budgets turn performance from a one-time project into a property maintained by every change. Tools: Lighthouse CI, `size-limit` and bundler budgets, SLO alerts, query-count assertions in integration tests.

## Interview questions

<Accordions>
  <Accordion title="Why are averages misleading for latency?">
    Latency distributions are skewed; a few very slow requests pull the mean far from the typical
    value while hiding how bad the slow cases are. Percentiles (p50, p95, p99) describe both the
    typical and the tail experience.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Explain tail latency amplification.">
    When a request fans out to N backends, it is as slow as the slowest. With a 1 percent chance of
    a slow response per call, a request making 100 calls sees at least one slow call about 63
    percent of the time, so backend p99 becomes the user's typical latency.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="What does Little's law say and how is it useful?">
    Items in a system equal arrival rate times time in system. It sizes concurrency, pools, and
    instances, and explains why a slowing dependency exhausts resources: same traffic, longer time
    per request, more requests in flight.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Why shouldn't a latency-sensitive service run at 95 percent CPU?">
    Queueing delay grows roughly as 1/(1 − utilisation). At 95 percent, average time in system is
    about 20 times the service time, and small slowdowns cause huge latency spikes. Headroom keeps
    queues short.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="What is Amdahl's law's practical lesson?">
    The overall gain from speeding up a part is capped by that part's share of total time. Profile
    first and optimize the largest contributor; optimizing a 10 percent step can never make the
    whole more than about 1.1 times faster.
  </Accordion>
</Accordions>
