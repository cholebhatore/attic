An **optimizer** is the rule that turns the gradients computed by [backpropagation](/ai/neural-networks/mlp-from-scratch) into an update of the parameters. Plain [gradient descent](/ai/neural-networks/gradient-descent) uses the simplest rule — step against the gradient, scaled by the learning rate — and on real networks it is slow and fragile. Every modern optimizer adds a small amount of memory, a few numbers stored per parameter between steps, and uses it to make better steps.

The optimizers form a lineage. Each fixes one specific weakness of the one before:

| Optimizer | Adds                                                  | Fixes                                                                       |
| :-------- | :---------------------------------------------------- | :-------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| SGD       | —                                                     | (the baseline)                                                              |
| Momentum  | A running sum of past gradients                       | Zig-zagging in narrow valleys; slow progress on flat stretches              |
| RMSprop   | A running average of squared gradients, per parameter | One learning rate cannot suit parameters with very different gradient sizes |
| Adam      | Both of the above, with bias correction               | Combines both fixes; the default for most models                            |
| AdamW     | Weight decay applied outside the adaptive scaling     | Regularization that Adam silently weakened                                  |

**Builds on:** [Gradient Descent](/ai/neural-networks/gradient-descent), especially the learning-rate stability analysis.

## The two problems with plain SGD

Plain SGD updates every parameter by $\theta \leftarrow \theta - \eta g$, where $g$ is its gradient. Two properties of real loss landscapes make this inefficient.

**1. Curvature differs wildly between directions.** The loss may curve sharply along some directions and gently along others. Take $L = \tfrac{1}{2}(p_1^2 + 100\,p_2^2)$: curvature 1 along $p_1$, 100 along $p_2$. [Stability](/ai/neural-networks/gradient-descent#the-learning-rate-decides-everything) requires $\eta < 2/100 = 0.02$, set by the **steepest** direction. At a safe $\eta = 0.001$, each step multiplies $p_2$'s distance to the minimum by $1 - 0.001 \times 100 = 0.9$ and $p_1$'s by $1 - 0.001 \times 1 = 0.999$. Shrinking each tenfold takes 22 steps for $p_2$ and 2,301 for $p_1$. The ratio of the largest to smallest curvature, the **condition number**, here 100, becomes a hundredfold slowdown.

**2. Gradient sizes differ between parameters and over time.** Different layers can have gradients orders of magnitude apart, and gradients shrink as training progresses. A single global learning rate is too big for some parameters and too small for others at any given moment.

Momentum addresses the first problem; adaptive methods address the second.

## Momentum

**Momentum** keeps a running, decaying sum of past gradients, called the **velocity** $\mathbf{v}$, and steps along it instead of along the current gradient:

$$
\mathbf{v} \leftarrow \mu\,\mathbf{v} + \mathbf{g}, \qquad \boldsymbol{\theta} \leftarrow \boldsymbol{\theta} - \eta\,\mathbf{v}
$$

$\mu$, typically 0.9, sets how much past velocity is kept. The physical picture is a heavy ball rolling down the landscape: it builds speed going consistently downhill, and its inertia carries it through small bumps.

The key effect: gradients that **keep pointing the same way add up**, and gradients that **alternate sign cancel**. With $\mu = 0.9$ and a gradient of magnitude 2:

| Step | Constant gradient $g = +2$: velocity | Alternating gradient $g = \pm 2$: velocity |
| ---: | -----------------------------------: | -----------------------------------------: |
|    1 |                                 2.00 |                                       2.00 |
|    2 |                                 3.80 |                                      −0.20 |
|    3 |                                 5.42 |                                       1.82 |
|   10 |                                13.03 |                                      −0.69 |
|   40 |                                19.70 |                                      −1.04 |

A consistent gradient is amplified toward $1/(1-\mu) = 10$ times its size; an oscillating one is damped to about $1/(1+\mu) \approx 0.53$ times. In a narrow valley, the steep cross-valley direction oscillates and is damped, while the gentle along-valley direction is consistent and is amplified — exactly the correction needed. Momentum also raises the stability limit: on the quadratic above, SGD diverges above $\eta = 0.02$, while momentum with $\mu = 0.9$ stays stable up to $2(1 + \mu)/100 = 0.038$.

On the same quadratic after 2,000 steps at $\eta = 0.001$, SGD reaches a loss of $9 \times 10^{-3}$; momentum reaches $2 \times 10^{-20}$.

**Nesterov momentum** evaluates the gradient at the point the velocity is about to carry the parameters to, rather than where they are now — a look-ahead that corrects overshoot earlier. It is usually slightly better than plain momentum and is a one-flag change (`nesterov=True`).

Momentum only helps where there is oscillation to cancel. On a perfectly round bowl, its inertia overshoots and plain SGD is faster. Neural network landscapes are far from round, so in practice momentum nearly always helps.

## Adaptive learning rates: AdaGrad and RMSprop

The second problem calls for a separate effective learning rate per parameter. The idea: divide each parameter's gradient by a measure of that parameter's typical gradient size, so every parameter takes steps of comparable size.

**AdaGrad** (Duchi et al., 2011) divides by the square root of the **sum** of all past squared gradients. Parameters with large gradients get small steps, rarely updated parameters get large ones — excellent for sparse features such as word embeddings. But the sum only grows, so the effective learning rate shrinks toward zero and training eventually stalls.

**RMSprop** (Hinton, 2012) replaces the sum with an exponential moving **average**, so old gradients are forgotten:

$$
s \leftarrow \alpha\, s + (1 - \alpha)\, g^2, \qquad \theta \leftarrow \theta - \eta \frac{g}{\sqrt{s} + \epsilon}
$$

$\sqrt{s}$ is the root mean square of recent gradients for that parameter, and $\epsilon \approx 10^{-8}$ prevents division by zero. On the quadratic above, the first-step gradients are $[1, 100]$; dividing each by its own size makes both steps equal to $\eta$. The hundredfold imbalance between directions disappears, and the steep direction no longer dictates the learning rate for the flat one.

## Adam

**Adam** (Kingma and Ba, 2014) combines momentum and RMSprop. It keeps two running averages per parameter: the **first moment** $m$, an average of gradients (direction, as in momentum), and the **second moment** $v$, an average of squared gradients (scale, as in RMSprop):

$$
\begin{aligned}
m &\leftarrow \beta_1 m + (1 - \beta_1)\, g && \beta_1 = 0.9,\ \text{about 10 steps of memory} \\
v &\leftarrow \beta_2 v + (1 - \beta_2)\, g^2 && \beta_2 = 0.999,\ \text{about 1,000 steps} \\
\hat{m} &= m / (1 - \beta_1^t), \quad \hat{v} = v / (1 - \beta_2^t) && \text{bias correction at step } t \\
\theta &\leftarrow \theta - \eta\, \frac{\hat{m}}{\sqrt{\hat{v}} + \epsilon}
\end{aligned}
$$

### Bias correction, traced by hand

Both averages start at 0. After the first step with gradient $g = 1$: $m = 0.1 \times 1 = 0.1$ and $v = 0.001 \times 1 = 0.001$. These are far too small, because 90% and 99.9% of each average is the initial zero. Without correction, the step would be $\eta \times 0.1/\sqrt{0.001} = 3.16\,\eta$ — over three times too large, at exactly the moment when the averages are least reliable.

The correction divides by $1 - \beta^t$, the total weight placed on real gradients so far: $\hat{m} = 0.1/0.1 = 1$ and $\hat{v} = 0.001/0.001 = 1$, giving a step of exactly $\eta$. As $t$ grows, $\beta^t \to 0$ and the correction fades away.

### What Adam's step looks like

- **The step size is about $\eta$, regardless of gradient size.** $\hat{m}/\sqrt{\hat{v}}$ is a ratio of a gradient average to a gradient magnitude, so it is dimensionless and at most about 1. Feed Adam gradients of $[1, 100]$ and both parameters move by $\eta$. In Adam, the learning rate is effectively a **distance per step**, not a multiplier on the gradient.
- **Scale invariance.** Multiplying the loss by 1,000 leaves Adam's updates unchanged. This makes Adam robust to badly scaled losses and gradient spikes.
- **Consistent gradients give full steps; noisy ones give small steps.** If a parameter's gradient keeps flipping sign, $m$ averages toward 0 while $v$ stays large, so the step shrinks automatically.

Defaults ($\eta = 10^{-3}$, $\beta_1 = 0.9$, $\beta_2 = 0.999$) work across a wide range of problems, which is a large part of Adam's popularity. Large language model training typically uses $\beta_2 = 0.95$, shortening the second-moment memory to about 20 steps so it reacts faster to gradient spikes.

## Weight decay, and why AdamW exists

**Weight decay** shrinks every weight toward zero a little each step, a form of [regularization](/ai/neural-networks/regularization#weight-decay-l2-regularization) that discourages large weights. There are two ways to implement it:

- **L2 regularization** adds $\tfrac{\lambda}{2}\lVert\theta\rVert^2$ to the loss, which adds $\lambda\theta$ to every gradient.
- **Decoupled decay** subtracts $\eta\lambda\theta$ from the weights directly, separately from the gradient step.

For plain SGD the two are identical. For Adam they are not, because the L2 term enters the gradient and is then divided by $\sqrt{\hat{v}}$ like everything else. Loshchilov and Hutter (2017) showed this breaks regularization. In the cleanest case, a quadratic whose gradient is proportional to the parameter, adding $\lambda\theta$ just rescales the gradient, and Adam is scale-invariant, so the decay **has no effect at all**. Measured over 200 steps from $\theta = 8$:

| Decay strength $\lambda$ | Adam + L2: final $\theta$ | AdamW: final $\theta$ |
| -----------------------: | ------------------------: | --------------------: |
|                        0 |                     7.801 |                 7.801 |
|                      0.1 |                     7.801 |                 7.645 |
|                      1.0 |                     7.801 |                 6.376 |

A tenfold change in $\lambda$ does nothing under Adam with L2. In real networks the effect is not zero but is distorted: parameters with large gradients are decayed less, which is backwards.

**AdamW** applies the decay outside the adaptive step:

$$
\theta \leftarrow \theta - \eta\left(\frac{\hat{m}}{\sqrt{\hat{v}} + \epsilon} + \lambda\,\theta\right)
$$

AdamW with $\lambda \approx 0.1$ is the standard optimizer for transformers. Weight decay is usually turned off for biases and normalization parameters, which do not benefit from it, by putting them in a separate parameter group.

## Lighter-weight variants

**Lion** (Chen et al., 2023) keeps only a momentum buffer and steps by the **sign** of an interpolation of momentum and gradient, so every parameter moves by exactly $\eta$ per step. It needs half of Adam's optimizer memory and matches AdamW on many vision and language tasks, but needs a learning rate 3 to 10 times smaller and a correspondingly larger weight decay.

**Adafactor** (Shazeer and Stern, 2018) saves memory on Adam's second moment. For a weight matrix of shape $(n, m)$ it stores per-row and per-column averages ($n + m$ numbers) instead of the full $(n \times m)$ matrix of squared-gradient averages, and reconstructs an approximation from their outer product. For a $4096 \times 4096$ matrix that is 8,192 numbers instead of 16.8 million. It trained T5.

## Memory cost

Optimizer state is stored in 32-bit floats, 4 bytes per number:

| Optimizer      | Values stored per parameter | Memory for a 7B-parameter model |
| :------------- | :-------------------------- | ------------------------------: |
| SGD            | 0                           |                            0 GB |
| SGD + momentum | 1                           |                           28 GB |
| Lion           | 1                           |                           28 GB |
| Adam / AdamW   | 2                           |                           56 GB |
| Adafactor      | ≈ 0 (factored)              |                          ≈ 0 GB |

In mixed-precision training, AdamW needs about 16 bytes per parameter in total — 4 for a float32 master copy of the weights, 4 for gradients, 8 for the two moments — so 112 GB for a 7B model before activations. Optimizer state is often the largest item, which is why distributed training shards it first (ZeRO, covered in [Scaling and Parallelism](/ai/llm/scaling-and-parallelism)).

## Choosing an optimizer

| Situation                                     | Optimizer and typical settings                                                                   |
| :-------------------------------------------- | :----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Transformers, pretraining or fine-tuning      | AdamW, $\eta = 3 \times 10^{-4}$ (lower for fine-tuning), $\lambda = 0.1$, $\beta = (0.9, 0.95)$ |
| CNN image classification, best final accuracy | SGD + Nesterov momentum 0.9, $\eta = 0.1$, $\lambda = 5 \times 10^{-4}$, cosine schedule         |
| Small models and quick experiments            | Adam, $\eta = 10^{-3}$                                                                           |
| Optimizer memory is the bottleneck            | Lion, Adafactor, or 8-bit Adam                                                                   |

Every choice is paired with a [learning rate schedule](/ai/neural-networks/lr-schedules), usually warmup followed by decay. SGD with momentum still tends to reach slightly better accuracy on image classification when carefully tuned; AdamW is clearly better for transformers and much less sensitive to its settings.

## Key terms

| Term                       | Meaning                                                                                 |
| :------------------------- | :-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **optimizer**              | The rule turning gradients into parameter updates                                       |
| **condition number**       | Ratio of largest to smallest curvature; how badly one learning rate fits all directions |
| **momentum / velocity**    | A decaying sum of past gradients used as the step direction                             |
| **adaptive learning rate** | Scaling each parameter's step by its own recent gradient size                           |
| **first / second moment**  | Running averages of the gradient / squared gradient in Adam                             |
| **bias correction**        | Dividing by $1 - \beta^t$ to undo the zero initialization of the running averages       |
| **weight decay**           | Shrinking weights toward zero each step                                                 |
| **decoupled weight decay** | Applying decay directly to the weights rather than through the gradient (AdamW)         |

## Interview questions

<Accordions>
  <Accordion title="What two problems of plain SGD do momentum and adaptive methods solve?">

    Momentum solves ill-conditioning: when curvature differs greatly between directions, the stable learning rate is set by the steepest one, so SGD zig-zags across the valley and crawls along it; momentum damps the oscillating direction and accelerates the consistent one. Adaptive methods solve differing gradient scales: they normalize each parameter's step by its own recent gradient magnitude, so one global learning rate suits all parameters.

  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Show numerically how momentum treats a constant versus an alternating gradient.">

    With $v \leftarrow 0.9v + g$ and $g = 2$, a constant gradient builds the velocity to 3.8, 5.42, … approaching $2/(1 - 0.9) = 20$, a tenfold amplification. An alternating $\pm 2$ gives 2, −0.2, 1.82, … settling near magnitude $2/(1 + 0.9) \approx 1.05$, roughly halved. Consistent directions are amplified and oscillating ones damped.

  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Why does Adam need bias correction? Work through the first step.">

    The moments start at 0, so after one step with $g = 1$, $m = 0.1$ and $v = 0.001$, biased toward zero. Uncorrected, the step is $\eta \cdot 0.1/\sqrt{0.001} = 3.16\eta$. Dividing by $1 - \beta_1 = 0.1$ and $1 - \beta_2 = 0.001$ gives $\hat{m} = \hat{v} = 1$ and a step of exactly $\eta$. The correction fades as $\beta^t \to 0$.

  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Why is Adam's learning rate a distance rather than a gain?">

    The update is $\eta\hat{m}/\sqrt{\hat{v}}$, and the ratio of an averaged gradient to its own root mean square is dimensionless and at most about 1. Each parameter therefore moves by at most about $\eta$ per step, regardless of how large its gradient is. In SGD the step is $\eta g$, so its size scales with the gradient.

  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Why does L2 regularization not work properly with Adam, and how does AdamW fix it?">

    L2 adds $\lambda\theta$ to the gradient, which Adam then divides by $\sqrt{\hat{v}}$. Parameters with large gradients receive less decay, and in the extreme case where the gradient is proportional to $\theta$, the added term is a pure rescaling that Adam's scale invariance removes completely. AdamW subtracts $\eta\lambda\theta$ directly, outside the adaptive normalization, so decay acts uniformly.

  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="How much optimizer memory does AdamW need for a 7B-parameter model?">

    Two float32 values per parameter, 8 bytes, so 56 GB for the moments alone. With a float32 master copy of the weights and float32 gradients in mixed precision, the total is about 16 bytes per parameter, 112 GB, before activations.

  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="When is SGD with momentum still preferred over Adam?">

    For CNN image classification when the goal is the best final accuracy and there is budget to tune the learning rate and schedule; well-tuned SGD often generalizes slightly better there. For transformers AdamW is clearly better and far more robust to its hyperparameters.

  </Accordion>
</Accordions>
