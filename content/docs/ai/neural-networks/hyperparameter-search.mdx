**Parameters** are the numbers training learns: weights and biases. **Hyperparameters** are the numbers chosen before training that no gradient ever updates: learning rate, batch size, weight decay, dropout rate, number of layers. Each choice of hyperparameters requires a full or partial training run to evaluate, called a **trial**, so the question is never "what is the right learning rate?" in the abstract. It is: given a fixed budget of trials, how should they be spent so the best one is as good as possible?

**Builds on:** [Optimizers](/ai/neural-networks/optimizers), [Learning Rate Schedules](/ai/neural-networks/lr-schedules), and [Overfitting and Regularization](/ai/neural-networks/regularization#training-validation-and-test-sets) — hyperparameters are always scored on the **validation** set, never the test set.

---

## Which hyperparameters matter

A small number of hyperparameters dominate. Searching them first and leaving the rest at standard defaults is the single most effective decision:

| Hyperparameter             | Search on     | Typical range          | Sensitivity   |
| :------------------------- | :------------ | :--------------------- | :------------ |
| Learning rate              | Log scale     | $10^{-5}$ to $10^{-1}$ | Very high     |
| Batch size                 | Powers of two | 16 to 4,096            | High          |
| Weight decay               | Log scale     | $10^{-6}$ to $10^{-1}$ | Medium        |
| Dropout                    | Linear        | 0 to 0.5               | Medium        |
| Warmup fraction            | Log scale     | 0.1% to 10% of steps   | Medium        |
| Adam $\beta_2$, $\epsilon$ | —             | Defaults               | Low           |
| Width, depth               | Integer       | Model-dependent        | Low to medium |

Two pairs are coupled, so changing one shifts the best value of the other. **Learning rate and batch size**: a larger batch tolerates a proportionally larger learning rate (the [linear scaling rule](/ai/neural-networks/gradient-descent#epochs-iterations-and-batch-size)). **Learning rate and weight decay** under AdamW: the decay applied per step is $\eta\lambda$, so halving the learning rate halves the effective decay.

## Grid search versus random search

**Grid search** picks $k$ values per hyperparameter and tries every combination. **Random search** samples each hyperparameter independently from its range for every trial. Bergstra and Bengio (2012) showed that random search is better at the same budget, for a reason that is pure counting.

With three hyperparameters and five values each, a grid costs $5^3 = 125$ trials and tests only **5 distinct learning rates**. Random search with the same 125 trials tests **125 distinct learning rates**, and 125 distinct values of every other hyperparameter too. Since the result usually depends strongly on one or two hyperparameters and weakly on the rest, the grid spends most of its budget varying unimportant settings while the important one is checked at only five points.

| Hyperparameters | Values each | Grid trials | Learning rates tested by grid | Learning rates tested by random (same budget) |
| --------------: | ----------: | ----------: | ----------------------------: | --------------------------------------------: |
|               2 |           5 |          25 |                             5 |                                            25 |
|               3 |           5 |         125 |                             5 |                                           125 |
|               5 |           3 |         243 |                             3 |                                           243 |

The grid's cost also grows exponentially with the number of hyperparameters; random search's does not. Grid search is only sensible for one or two hyperparameters.

### Sample on a log scale

The learning rate ranges over orders of magnitude, and what matters is its order of magnitude: $10^{-4}$ versus $10^{-3}$ is as large a difference as $10^{-3}$ versus $10^{-2}$. Sampling **uniformly** between $10^{-5}$ and $10^{-1}$ would put 90% of trials between $10^{-2}$ and $10^{-1}$ and almost none below $10^{-3}$. Sampling **log-uniformly** — draw the exponent uniformly between −5 and −1, then compute $10^{\text{exponent}}$ — gives each decade an equal share of trials. The same applies to weight decay, warmup length, and any hyperparameter whose plausible range spans several factors of ten.

## Stopping bad trials early

Most trials are visibly bad within a few epochs. Running them to completion wastes the budget. **Successive halving** gives every configuration a small budget, keeps the best fraction, gives the survivors more, and repeats.

With 81 configurations, a full run of 81 epochs, and a keep-fraction of one third:

| Round | Configurations | Epochs each | Cost (configuration-epochs) |
| ----: | -------------: | ----------: | --------------------------: |
|     1 |             81 |           1 |                          81 |
|     2 |             27 |           3 |                          81 |
|     3 |              9 |           9 |                          81 |
|     4 |              3 |          27 |                          81 |
|     5 |              1 |          81 |                          81 |
|       |                |   **Total** |                     **405** |

405 configuration-epochs is the cost of just five full training runs, but it screened 81 candidates. The risk is that a configuration that starts slowly but would finish best — often one with a smaller learning rate — is eliminated too early.

**Hyperband** (Li et al., 2017) hedges against that risk by running several successive-halving schedules ("brackets") with different starting points: one that screens 81 configurations from 1 epoch, one that screens 34 from 3 epochs, and so on, down to one that runs 5 configurations at the full 81 epochs with no pruning. Each bracket costs about the same, and the best result across all brackets wins. It removes the need to guess how aggressive pruning should be.

**ASHA** (Asynchronous Successive Halving) promotes a configuration to the next round as soon as it ranks in the top fraction of those reported so far, instead of waiting for the whole round to finish. On a cluster this keeps every worker busy, at the cost of occasionally promoting on incomplete evidence. It is the standard choice for parallel search.

## Model-based search

Random search chooses every trial independently. **Model-based** (or **Bayesian**) search uses the results of completed trials to choose the next one, by fitting a cheap **surrogate model** of how the score depends on the hyperparameters.

- **Gaussian-process methods** model the score directly, with a predicted mean and an uncertainty at every untried configuration, and choose the configuration with the highest **expected improvement** over the best so far. A candidate scores well either because it is predicted to be good or because it is highly uncertain, which balances exploiting known good regions against exploring unknown ones. They work best in low-dimensional continuous spaces.
- **TPE** (Tree-structured Parzen Estimator), Optuna's default, splits completed trials into a "good" group (say, the best 25%) and a "bad" group, estimates how densely each group populates every region of hyperparameter space, and tries next where good trials are dense and bad ones sparse. It handles categorical and conditional hyperparameters naturally.

Both need data before their models are meaningful. With fewer than about 20 trials, random search is as good or better; above that, model-based search pulls ahead by avoiding regions that have already scored badly.

**Population-based training** (PBT; Jaderberg et al., 2017) works differently: it trains a population of models in parallel and periodically replaces the worst performers with copies of the best, with their hyperparameters perturbed by a random factor of about 0.8 to 1.2. The result is a hyperparameter **schedule** discovered during a single round of training. It needs many parallel workers and is used mostly in reinforcement learning and long training runs.

## Optuna in practice

```python title="optuna_search.py"
import optuna

def objective(trial):
    lr = trial.suggest_float("lr", 1e-5, 1e-1, log=True)        # log-uniform
    wd = trial.suggest_float("weight_decay", 1e-6, 1e-1, log=True)
    dropout = trial.suggest_float("dropout", 0.0, 0.5)
    model, optimizer = build(lr=lr, weight_decay=wd, dropout=dropout)

    for epoch in range(num_epochs):
        train_one_epoch(model, optimizer)
        val_loss = evaluate(model)
        trial.report(val_loss, epoch)          # lets the pruner see progress
        if trial.should_prune():
            raise optuna.TrialPruned()
    return val_loss

study = optuna.create_study(
    direction="minimize",
    sampler=optuna.samplers.TPESampler(seed=0, n_startup_trials=10),
    pruner=optuna.pruners.HyperbandPruner(),
    storage="sqlite:///study.db", study_name="run1", load_if_exists=True,
)
study.optimize(objective, n_trials=100)
print(study.best_params)
```

The **sampler** decides which configuration to try next (random, TPE); the **pruner** decides whether a running trial should be stopped (median, Hyperband). They are independent choices. `trial.report` is what makes pruning possible. Setting a seed makes the study reproducible, and storage lets a crashed search resume. Ray Tune offers the same methods for distributed search, with `ASHAScheduler` and `PopulationBasedTraining`.

## A protocol that works

<Steps>
<Step>

**Fix everything else.** Use a known-good architecture, data pipeline, optimizer, and schedule shape, with standard defaults for everything low on the sensitivity table.

</Step>
<Step>

**Find the learning rate's order of magnitude.** About 20 short trials, log-uniform from $10^{-5}$ to $10^{-1}$. This also confirms the model trains at all.

</Step>
<Step>

**Add the next most important hyperparameter**, usually weight decay or batch size, around the best learning rate.

</Step>
<Step>

**Search jointly** over the four or five important hyperparameters with TPE and a pruner, 50 to 200 trials.

</Step>
<Step>

**Re-run the top three configurations with two or three different random seeds.** The variation between seeds is often larger than the difference between the top configurations. A winner by less than that spread is not a real winner. Report mean and standard deviation, not a single best run.

</Step>
</Steps>

Budget matters more than algorithm: going from 20 trials to 200 helps more than switching from random search to TPE, and adding pruning, which multiplies how many configurations a budget can screen, helps more than both.

## Key terms

| Term                          | Meaning                                                                                       |
| :---------------------------- | :-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **hyperparameter**            | A setting chosen before training and not updated by gradients                                 |
| **trial**                     | One training run with one hyperparameter configuration                                        |
| **grid / random search**      | Every combination of a fixed set of values / independent random samples                       |
| **log-uniform sampling**      | Sampling the exponent uniformly so each order of magnitude is equally covered                 |
| **successive halving**        | Train many configurations briefly, keep the best fraction, give survivors more budget, repeat |
| **Hyperband / ASHA**          | Several halving schedules side by side / asynchronous halving for parallel workers            |
| **surrogate model**           | A cheap model of score as a function of hyperparameters, used to choose the next trial        |
| **TPE**                       | Choose where good trials are dense relative to bad ones                                       |
| **population-based training** | Evolve hyperparameters during training by copying and perturbing the best workers             |

## Interview questions

<Accordions>
  <Accordion title="Why does random search beat grid search at the same budget?">

    A grid with $k$ values per hyperparameter tests each one at only $k$ distinct values, however many combinations it runs: 125 trials over three hyperparameters test five learning rates. Random search with 125 trials tests 125 distinct values of every hyperparameter. Because performance usually depends mostly on one or two hyperparameters, random search covers the important ones far better.

  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Why sample the learning rate log-uniformly?">

    Its effect depends on its order of magnitude, and its range spans several. Uniform sampling between $10^{-5}$ and $10^{-1}$ puts 90% of samples in the top decade and almost none below $10^{-3}$. Log-uniform sampling draws the exponent uniformly, giving every decade equal coverage.

  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Explain successive halving with numbers, and its main risk.">

    Start with 81 configurations at 1 epoch, keep the best third and triple the budget: 27 at 3 epochs, 9 at 9, 3 at 27, 1 at 81. Each round costs 81 configuration-epochs, so screening 81 candidates costs 405, the price of five full runs. The risk is eliminating slow starters that would have finished best; Hyperband hedges by also running less aggressive brackets.

  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="When should model-based search such as TPE be used instead of random search?">

    When the budget exceeds roughly 20 to 30 trials. Model-based methods need enough completed trials to fit a useful surrogate; before that they model noise. With more trials they stop revisiting regions that already scored poorly. TPE also handles categorical and conditional hyperparameters.

  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Why should the top configurations be re-run with different seeds?">

    The variation in final score from random initialization and data order is often larger than the gap between the top few configurations. A configuration that wins on one seed by less than that spread may lose on the next. Re-running and reporting the mean and standard deviation shows whether the winner is real.

  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Which hyperparameters are coupled, and what does that imply for search?">

    Learning rate and batch size (larger batches tolerate proportionally larger learning rates), and learning rate and weight decay under AdamW (the per-step decay is their product). Tuning one while the other is fixed at an unsuitable value gives a misleading optimum, so coupled pairs should be searched together, or one re-tuned whenever the other changes.

  </Accordion>
</Accordions>
