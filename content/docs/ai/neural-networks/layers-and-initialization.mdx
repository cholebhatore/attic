**Weight initialization** is the choice of the random values every weight starts from before training. It sounds like a detail, but in a deep network it decides whether training works at all. A network is a long chain of multiplications. If each layer slightly shrinks the signal passing through it, forty layers shrink it to nothing; if each slightly enlarges it, forty layers overflow. Gradients suffer the same fate on the way back. Good initialization sets the scale of the weights so that every layer passes its input on at roughly the same size.

This page explains the two things an initialization must get right — breaking symmetry and setting the scale — and derives the two standard answers, **Xavier** and **He** initialization.

**Builds on:** [Backpropagation](/ai/neural-networks/mlp-from-scratch) and [Neurons and Activations](/ai/neural-networks/neurons-and-activations). Basic facts about **variance**, the average squared distance from the mean, are in [Descriptive Statistics](/ai/math-foundations/descriptive-statistics).

## Requirement 1: break the symmetry

Suppose every weight in a hidden layer starts at the same value — zero, or 0.5. Then every hidden neuron computes the same function of the input, produces the same output, and, by the backpropagation rules, receives the same gradient. After the update, all the weights are still equal to each other. They stay equal forever. A layer of 256 identical neurons is no more expressive than a layer of one.

Starting with all weights at exactly zero is worse still for ReLU networks: every pre-activation is 0, every output is 0, and with weights of 0 above them no gradient flows back at all.

**Random** initialization breaks the symmetry: each neuron starts at a different point and so learns a different feature. That settles that weights must be random. Biases do not need to be: they are usually initialized to 0, because the random weights already make neurons differ.

The remaining question is: random with what spread?

---

## Requirement 2: get the scale right

### One layer

Consider one output of a linear layer with $n$ inputs, $y = \sum_{i=1}^{n} w_i x_i$, where the weights are random with mean 0 and variance $\text{Var}(w)$, independent of the inputs. Variances of independent terms add, so:

$$
\text{Var}(y) = n \cdot \text{Var}(w) \cdot \text{Var}(x)
$$

$n$, the number of inputs feeding each output, is called the **fan-in**. The factor $n\,\text{Var}(w)$ is the layer's **gain**: how much it multiplies the variance passing through.

This explains why the spread of the weights has to depend on the layer's width. Adding $n$ random-signed numbers gives a sum whose typical size grows like $\sqrt{n}$, because positive and negative terms partly cancel:

| Number of terms $n$ | Typical size (standard deviation) of the sum of $n$ standard-normal terms | $\sqrt{n}$ |
| ------------------: | ------------------------------------------------------------------------: | ---------: |
|                   4 |                                                                      2.00 |       2.00 |
|                  64 |                                                                      7.98 |       8.00 |
|                 256 |                                                                     15.98 |      16.00 |

To keep the output the same size as the input, the gain must be 1, so $\text{Var}(w) = 1/n$: each weight's standard deviation must shrink as $1/\sqrt{n}$. A wider layer needs smaller weights.

**Worked example.** A layer with fan-in 8, input variance 7.5, and 256 outputs:

| Weight std              | $\text{Var}(w)$ | Gain $8 \cdot \text{Var}(w)$ | Predicted output variance | Measured |
| :---------------------- | --------------: | ---------------------------: | ------------------------: | -------: |
| 0.1 ("start small")     |           0.010 |                         0.08 |                      0.60 |     0.61 |
| $1/\sqrt{8} = 0.354$    |           0.125 |                         1.00 |                      7.50 |     7.58 |
| 1.0 ("standard normal") |           1.000 |                         8.00 |                     60.00 |    60.61 |

Only $1/\sqrt{\text{fan-in}}$ leaves the signal's size unchanged.

### Many layers: gains multiply

Stack $L$ layers and the variance at the output is the input variance times the product of every layer's gain. With a constant gain $g$:

$$
\text{Var}(\text{output}) = g^{L} \cdot \text{Var}(\text{input})
$$

Any gain other than 1 is compounded exponentially. Over 40 layers, $g = 0.5$ gives $10^{-12}$ and $g = 2$ gives $10^{12}$. The signal arriving at the last layer is either indistinguishable from zero or overflowing — and the gradients flowing backward are multiplied by the same kind of factors, so they [vanish or explode](/ai/classical-deep-learning/vanishing-gradients) in the same way. Training cannot recover from either.

---

## Xavier initialization

Glorot and Bengio (2010) applied this analysis to both directions. In the forward pass, keeping activations stable needs $n_{\text{in}}\text{Var}(w) = 1$. In the backward pass, each input's gradient is a sum over the layer's $n_{\text{out}}$ outputs, so keeping gradients stable needs $n_{\text{out}}\text{Var}(w) = 1$. Both cannot hold unless the layer is square, so **Xavier** (or **Glorot**) initialization takes the compromise:

$$
\text{Var}(w) = \frac{2}{n_{\text{in}} + n_{\text{out}}}
$$

For a square layer this is exactly $1/n$. The derivation assumes the activation is roughly linear and symmetric around zero, which holds for `tanh` near its centre. Xavier is the right choice for `tanh` and sigmoid networks and for linear layers.

---

## He initialization

ReLU breaks Xavier's assumption. It zeroes every negative pre-activation, so for symmetric, zero-centred pre-activations it removes half the variance (more precisely, half the mean square) at every layer. Xavier's gain of 1 becomes $\tfrac{1}{2}$ per ReLU layer, and 40 layers lose a factor of $10^{12}$.

He et al. (2015) fixed it by doubling the variance to cancel the ReLU's halving:

$$
\text{Var}(w) = \frac{2}{n_{\text{in}}}
$$

This **He** (or **Kaiming**) initialization is the standard for ReLU-family networks, including GELU and SiLU, which behave similarly enough. It was what made training very deep plain CNNs from scratch possible.

### Measured: forty layers deep

A 40-layer network of width 256 with ReLU, input variance 1. The variance (mean square) of the activations leaving selected layers:

| Initialization | Gain per layer | Layer 1 |             Layer 10 |             Layer 20 |              Layer 40 |
| :------------- | -------------: | ------: | -------------------: | -------------------: | --------------------: |
| He ($2/n$)     |              1 |    1.00 |                 1.03 |                 1.33 |                  1.38 |
| Xavier ($1/n$) |            1/2 |    0.50 | $1.0 \times 10^{-3}$ | $1.3 \times 10^{-6}$ | $1.3 \times 10^{-12}$ |
| Std 0.01       |         0.0128 |   0.013 |           $10^{-19}$ |           $10^{-38}$ |            $10^{-76}$ |
| Std 1.0        |            128 |     128 |            $10^{21}$ |            $10^{42}$ |             $10^{84}$ |

Every row follows its predicted gain. Only He keeps the signal within a factor of two of its starting size. "Start small" (std 0.01) is catastrophic: the values fall below what `float32` can represent. The backward pass shows the same pattern, gradients at layer 1 relative to layer 40 shrinking by the same per-layer factors. (He's backward gain measures about 1.09 per layer rather than exactly 1, because the derivation assumes the ReLU mask is independent of the gradient, which is only approximately true. Over 40 layers that compounds to a harmless 30× growth.)

## Uniform or normal

Each scheme comes in a normal and a uniform version with the same variance. A uniform distribution on $[-a, a]$ has variance $a^2 / 3$, so a target standard deviation $\sigma$ corresponds to the bound $a = \sqrt{3}\,\sigma$:

| Scheme | Normal: standard deviation                    | Uniform: bound $a$                            |
| :----- | :-------------------------------------------- | :-------------------------------------------- |
| Xavier | $\sqrt{2 / (n_{\text{in}} + n_{\text{out}})}$ | $\sqrt{6 / (n_{\text{in}} + n_{\text{out}})}$ |
| He     | $\sqrt{2 / n_{\text{in}}}$                    | $\sqrt{6 / n_{\text{in}}}$                    |

Worked for the first layer of a $784 \to 256$ classifier: He normal uses std $\sqrt{2/784} = 0.0505$; Xavier uniform uses bound $\sqrt{6/1040} = 0.0760$. The choice between normal and uniform makes no practical difference.

---

## What PyTorch actually does

`nn.Linear` and `nn.Conv2d` use neither scheme by default. They call `kaiming_uniform_(weight, a=sqrt(5))`, which works out to a uniform distribution on $[-1/\sqrt{n}, 1/\sqrt{n}]$:

$$
\text{Var}(w) = \frac{(1/\sqrt{n})^2}{3} = \frac{1}{3n}
$$

That is a third of Xavier and a sixth of He. With ReLU, each layer's gain is $n \cdot \frac{1}{3n} \cdot \frac{1}{2} = \frac{1}{6}$. A 40-layer plain ReLU stack with default initialization shrinks its activations to about $10^{-31}$ of the input.

It works anyway in most real models, for two reasons. Shallow networks of two or three layers lose only a factor of 36 to 200, which training easily absorbs. And most deep architectures include [normalization layers](/ai/neural-networks/normalization), which rescale activations at every block, and [residual connections](/ai/classical-deep-learning/vision-architectures#resnet-the-residual-connection), which give the signal a path around the multiplications. Together these make the initial scale far less critical, which is why transformers train fine with the default.

To use He initialization explicitly:

```python
import torch.nn as nn

layer = nn.Linear(256, 256)
nn.init.kaiming_normal_(layer.weight, nonlinearity="relu")   # std = sqrt(2 / 256)
nn.init.zeros_(layer.bias)
```

## Diagnosing a scale problem

If a deep network without normalization will not train, measure the activation scale at each layer with forward hooks before tuning anything else:

| Mean square across layers            | Meaning               | Fix                                              |
| :----------------------------------- | :-------------------- | :----------------------------------------------- |
| Roughly flat (within a factor of 2)  | Gain about 1; healthy | None                                             |
| Falls by a constant factor per layer | Gain below 1          | Increase the std toward $\sqrt{2/n}$ for ReLU    |
| Grows by a constant factor per layer | Gain above 1          | Decrease the std; check for a missing activation |

A constant ratio between consecutive layers is a gain that is not 1, and no learning rate can fix that.

## What to use

| Network                                    | Initialization                                            |
| :----------------------------------------- | :-------------------------------------------------------- |
| MLP or CNN with ReLU, GELU, or SiLU        | He (`kaiming_normal_` with `nonlinearity="relu"`)         |
| Network with `tanh` or sigmoid activations | Xavier (`xavier_normal_`)                                 |
| Transformers (residual + normalization)    | PyTorch default or a small normal (std 0.02, as in GPT-2) |
| Biases                                     | Zero                                                      |
| A new head on a pretrained backbone        | Default or Xavier on the head; backbone keeps its weights |

Two architecture-specific adjustments are common. GPT-2 scales the initialization of each residual block's output projection by $1/\sqrt{2L}$ for $L$ layers, so the sum of many residual branches does not grow with depth. And LSTMs initialize the forget-gate bias to 1 or 2 so memory is kept by default (see [LSTM and GRU](/ai/classical-deep-learning/lstm-and-gru#practical-details)).

## Key terms

| Term                                  | Meaning                                                                             |
| :------------------------------------ | :---------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **symmetry breaking**                 | Random initialization so neurons start different and learn different features       |
| **fan-in / fan-out**                  | Number of inputs to / outputs from a unit in a layer                                |
| **gain**                              | The factor $n\,\text{Var}(w)$ by which a layer multiplies the variance of its input |
| **Xavier (Glorot)**                   | $\text{Var}(w) = 2/(n_{\text{in}} + n_{\text{out}})$, for `tanh` and linear layers  |
| **He (Kaiming)**                      | $\text{Var}(w) = 2/n_{\text{in}}$, compensating for ReLU halving the variance       |
| **vanishing / exploding activations** | Signal variance shrinking or growing exponentially with depth                       |

## Interview questions

<Accordions>
  <Accordion title="Why can all weights not be initialized to the same value?">

    Every neuron in a layer would compute the same output and receive the same gradient, so after every update the weights would remain equal and the layer would behave like a single neuron. Random initialization breaks this symmetry. With all-zero weights in a ReLU network, no gradient flows at all.

  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Derive the variance of a linear layer's output.">

    For $y = \sum_{i=1}^{n} w_i x_i$ with independent, zero-mean weights and inputs, the variance of each product is $\text{Var}(w)\text{Var}(x)$, and variances of independent terms add, so $\text{Var}(y) = n\,\text{Var}(w)\,\text{Var}(x)$. Keeping $\text{Var}(y) = \text{Var}(x)$ requires $\text{Var}(w) = 1/n$.

  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="What is the difference between Xavier and He initialization, and where does the factor of 2 come from?">

    Xavier sets $\text{Var}(w) = 2/(n_{\text{in}} + n_{\text{out}})$, balancing forward and backward variance for roughly linear activations such as `tanh`. He sets $\text{Var}(w) = 2/n_{\text{in}}$. ReLU zeroes the negative half of symmetric pre-activations, halving their mean square at every layer; doubling the weight variance restores a gain of 1.

  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="What happens to a 40-layer ReLU network initialized with standard deviation 0.01 at width 256?">

    The per-layer gain is $256 \times 10^{-4} \times 0.5 = 0.0128$, so after 40 layers the activation variance is about $10^{-76}$ of the input, far below what `float32` represents. Gradients vanish the same way. "Starting small" is not the same as starting at the right scale.

  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="What does nn.Linear initialize to by default, and why does it usually work?">

    Uniform on $[-1/\sqrt{n}, 1/\sqrt{n}]$, variance $1/(3n)$: a ReLU gain of $1/6$ per layer. Shallow networks lose only a small factor that training absorbs, and deep modern architectures use normalization and residual connections, which rescale and bypass the multiplications so the initial scale matters much less.

  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Why do biases not need random initialization?">

    Symmetry is already broken by the random weights, so neurons differ even with equal biases. Zero biases also keep pre-activations centred at initialization. Exceptions exist where a particular starting behaviour is wanted, such as an LSTM's forget-gate bias set to 1 so memory is retained by default.

  </Accordion>
</Accordions>
