The learning rate is the one <Tip term="hyperparameter">A setting you pick before training rather than one the training loop learns from data. The learning rate, its warmup length, and its decay factor are hyperparameters; the weights are parameters.</Tip> that has to change during a run. A constant value is either too large at the start, before the optimizer has any idea which direction is downhill, or too large at the end, when it overshoots a minimum it has already found. Schedules exist to be small at both ends and large in the middle.

Six families cover everything in use. This page gives the formula, the measured curve, and the situation each one belongs to.

---

## The plain-language version

The **learning rate** is the size of each step the optimizer takes downhill on the loss. A **learning rate schedule** is a rule that changes that step size as training goes on, instead of keeping it fixed.

Think of parking a car. You start slowly while you get your bearings, drive at full speed on the open road, and slow right down as you pull into the space. Training wants the same shape:

- **Small at the start (warmup)**: the model's first gradients are unreliable, and big steps on them can wreck the weights.
- **Large in the middle**: make fast progress.
- **Small at the end (decay)**: settle precisely into a good spot instead of bouncing around it.

| Word                           | Plain meaning                                                                                                                               |
| :----------------------------- | :------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **learning rate (`lr`)**       | The multiplier on each update: `new weight = old weight - lr * gradient` for plain gradient descent.                                        |
| **step**                       | One optimizer update, after one batch.                                                                                                      |
| **schedule**                   | A function from step number to learning rate.                                                                                               |
| **peak learning rate**         | The highest value the schedule reaches. Usually the number people mean when they say "the learning rate".                                   |
| **warmup**                     | A short opening phase where the rate climbs from near zero to its peak.                                                                     |
| **decay**                      | The phase where the rate shrinks toward its final value.                                                                                    |
| **step decay**                 | Drop the rate by a fixed factor (say 10x) at chosen milestones.                                                                             |
| **exponential decay**          | Multiply the rate by the same factor (like 0.99) every step.                                                                                |
| **cosine annealing**           | Lower the rate along a half cosine wave: slowly at first, faster in the middle, slowly again at the end. "Annealing" means gradual cooling. |
| **inverse square root (Noam)** | Warm up, then shrink in proportion to `1 / sqrt(step)`. Used by the original transformer paper.                                             |
| **WSD (warmup-stable-decay)**  | Warm up, hold the rate flat for most of training, then decay quickly at the end.                                                            |
| **restarts (SGDR)**            | Jump the rate back up periodically and decay again.                                                                                         |
| **divergence**                 | Training blows up: the loss shoots up or becomes `nan`. Usually a learning rate that is too high.                                           |
| **plateau**                    | The loss stops improving for a long stretch.                                                                                                |
| **scheduler**                  | The PyTorch object (`torch.optim.lr_scheduler`) that updates the optimizer's learning rate each step.                                       |

## Start here

Picture walking down a hillside in thick fog. You cannot see the valley, so you feel the slope under your feet and take a step. The learning rate is the length of that step. Too short and you spend the whole day on the mountain. Too long and one stride carries you clean across the valley and lands you higher up the far side than you started.

Everything on this page follows from one awkward fact: the right step length is not a constant. On the open hillside above the valley you want long strides. Down in the valley, where the ground is nearly flat and one careless step puts you back on the slope you just came down, you want short ones. A <Tip term="schedule">A rule that says what the learning rate is at every step of the run, usually written as a formula in the step counter t. "Decay schedule" and "LR schedule" mean the same thing.</Tip> is that rule written down.

### Every schedule is one line

Whatever the page looks like later, every family here fills in the same expression:

$$
\text{lr}(t) = \text{base} \times m(t)
$$

Read it as: "the rate at step $t$ equals the peak rate times a multiplier between zero and one." `base` never changes during the run; the entire personality of a schedule lives in $m(t)$, the shape. Warmup, cosine, WSD, Noam, SGDR — five different ways of drawing $m(t)$. This is not a simplification for the sake of teaching: it is exactly what PyTorch's `LambdaLR` asks you to return, which is why one function of `step` implements all of them (see Implementing it, below).

The knobs that appear in every formula below:

| Knob              | Written as | Plain meaning                                                   |
| :---------------- | :--------- | :-------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Peak rate         | `base`     | the largest rate the run ever uses                              |
| Shape             | `m(t)`     | the fraction of `base` used at step `t`, always between 0 and 1 |
| Warmup length     | `W`        | how many steps it takes to climb up to the peak                 |
| Total steps       | `T`        | when the run ends                                               |
| Decay factor      | `gamma`    | how much of the rate survives each interval                     |
| Floor             | `eta_min`  | the smallest rate the schedule is allowed to reach, usually 0   |
| Stable hold point | `hold`     | the step where a WSD run stops being flat and starts annealing  |

### A ten-step schedule you can check by hand

Take `base = 1e-3`, warmup `W = 2` steps, total `T = 10` steps, cosine decay after the warmup. Every number below is that formula evaluated step by step:

```text
t=0  lr=5.000000e-04  frac=0.5000  warmup
t=1  lr=1.000000e-03  frac=1.0000  warmup
t=2  lr=1.000000e-03  frac=1.0000  cosine
t=3  lr=9.619398e-04  frac=0.9619  cosine
t=4  lr=8.535534e-04  frac=0.8536  cosine
t=5  lr=6.913417e-04  frac=0.6913  cosine
t=6  lr=5.000000e-04  frac=0.5000  cosine
t=7  lr=3.086583e-04  frac=0.3087  cosine
t=8  lr=1.464466e-04  frac=0.1464  cosine
t=9  lr=3.806023e-05  frac=0.0381  cosine
```

The `frac` column is $m(t)$, the multiplier. Four things to notice:

- **Step 0 is not zero.** `lr(0)` is `5e-4`, half the peak, because the warmup formula uses `(t + 1) / W` and not `t / W`. With a plain `t / W` the first step of the run would do nothing at all.
- **The peak lands exactly at `t = W`.** Step 1 finishes the ramp and step 2 is the first full-rate step.
- **The midpoint is exactly half the peak.** At `t = 6` the anneal is half finished, `cos(pi * 0.5) = 0`, so $m = 0.5 \times (1 + 0) = 0.5$ and the rate is `5.000000e-04`. That is the one property worth memorizing about cosine.
- **From `t = 6` to `t = 9` the rate falls off a cliff.** It drops from half the peak to under 4% of it. Whatever a run is going to gain from annealing, it gains in that last stretch.

### The two failures, and why one shape fixes both

A schedule has to solve two unrelated problems, one at each end:

- **Early: a full-size step on bad information diverges.** The parameters are random and the <Tip term="second-moment estimate">In Adam and AdamW, a running average `v` of the squares of recent gradients. It is what divides the gradient to make each parameter's step its own size.</Tip> that Adam uses to size steps starts at zero, so the first few updates have enormous and meaningless magnitude. Warmup keeps those steps tiny until `v` has real statistics behind it.
- **Late: a full-size step will not let you stop.** Near a minimum the gradient is small and noisy, so a big rate makes the parameters orbit the minimum forever instead of settling in. <Tip term="Annealing">Borrowed from metallurgy: cooling a melt slowly so it settles into a good crystal structure. In training it means shrinking the learning rate toward the end of a run so the parameters come to rest instead of jittering.</Tip> shrinks the steps and recovers accuracy you would otherwise leave on the table.

Those are the only two jobs. Every family below is an answer to "how do I get small-big-small, and what else do I want along the way?"

## Why the rate has to move

Two separate problems, solved by two separate halves of the schedule.

**Early in training** the parameters are random and the gradient points in a direction that is only loosely related to where the minimum is. Adam compounds this, because its second-moment estimate `v` starts at zero and takes hundreds of steps to become a reliable average. Taking full-size steps on that information throws the parameters somewhere worse. <Tip term="Warmup">Starting a run at a small fraction of the peak rate and ramping linearly up to the peak over the first `W` steps, so the earliest updates are deliberately under-sized.</Tip> exists to keep the early steps small until the optimizer has statistics worth trusting.

How bad is "full-size" early? Adam's update is `lr * m / (sqrt(v) + eps)`, and before `v` has accumulated, that ratio is roughly `±1` per coordinate, so each weight moves about one `lr` in a direction it has no business picking. Across a single `512`-wide layer that is a displacement of about `1e-3 * sqrt(512) = 2.2627e-02` — a random shove of roughly `0.023` in a parameter space whose useful scale is a few thousandths. Run the same step under the warmup of the table above, where `lr(0) = 1e-6`, and the shove is `1e-6 * sqrt(512) = 2.2627e-05`, a thousand times smaller. Warmup does not make the first steps useless; it makes them harmless.

**Late in training** the parameters are near a minimum and the gradient is small but noisy. A large learning rate makes the parameters orbit the minimum rather than settle into it. Decay shrinks the steps so the final position is close to the best one the run visited. This is worth a surprising amount: on most tasks, annealing to zero recovers one to three tenths of a percent of accuracy over stopping at the peak rate.

That is the whole reason schedules exist. The rest of this page is shape.

## Warmup

The failure warmup prevents is early divergence: full-size steps taken before the optimizer's gradient statistics mean anything.

Linear warmup ramps the rate from zero to the base value over the first `W` steps:

```text
lr(t) = base * (t + 1) / W        for t < W
```

Read it as: "at step `t` you get the fraction `(t + 1) / W` of the peak rate." `W` is the knob that decides how long the optimizer is allowed to crawl. With `W = 1000` and `base = 1e-3`, step 0 gets `1.000e-06`, step 500 gets `5.010e-04`, and step 999 gets `1.000e-03` — the peak.

Using `t + 1` rather than `t` means step 0 gets a nonzero rate. With plain `t / W` the first step does nothing at all, which wastes it.

Typical warmup lengths:

| Model                 | Warmup                |
| :-------------------- | :-------------------- |
| Transformer, original | 4000 steps            |
| BERT base             | 10% of training steps |
| GPT-2, GPT-3          | 375 to 2000 steps     |
| LLaMA                 | 2000 steps            |
| CNN on ImageNet       | 5 epochs              |
| LoRA fine-tune        | 3% to 10% of steps    |

The rule of thumb is 1% to 10% of total steps. Warmup matters more for large learning rates, for Adam-family optimizers, and for models with normalization layers whose running statistics have not settled yet.

## The decay families

All six, measured at base `1e-3`, total `1000` steps, warmup `100`:

| Step `t` | Warmup + cosine | Step decay  | Exponential | Linear to zero | Inverse sqrt | WSD         |
| :------- | :-------------- | :---------- | :---------- | :------------- | :----------- | :---------- |
| 0        | `1.000e-05`     | `1.000e-03` | `1.000e-03` | `1.000e-05`    | `1.000e-05`  | `1.000e-05` |
| 1        | `2.000e-05`     | `1.000e-03` | `9.990e-04` | `2.000e-05`    | `2.000e-05`  | `2.000e-05` |
| 50       | `5.100e-04`     | `1.000e-03` | `9.512e-04` | `5.100e-04`    | `5.100e-04`  | `5.100e-04` |
| 100      | `1.000e-03`     | `1.000e-03` | `9.048e-04` | `1.000e-03`    | `1.000e-03`  | `1.000e-03` |
| 250      | `9.330e-04`     | `5.000e-04` | `7.787e-04` | `8.333e-04`    | `6.325e-04`  | `1.000e-03` |
| 500      | `5.868e-04`     | `2.500e-04` | `6.064e-04` | `5.556e-04`    | `4.472e-04`  | `1.000e-03` |
| 750      | `1.786e-04`     | `1.250e-04` | `4.722e-04` | `2.778e-04`    | `3.651e-04`  | `8.333e-04` |
| 999      | `3.046e-09`     | `1.250e-04` | `3.681e-04` | `1.111e-06`    | `3.164e-04`  | `3.333e-06` |

This table is six of the seven families from the 10000-step table above, squeezed to `T = 1000` (SGDR has no fixed `T` to squeeze). Schedules are shape-invariant: `t = 500` here is the halfway point of the run, and `t = 5000` above is also the halfway point, and both give the warmup+cosine rate `5.868e-04` and the WSD rate `1.000e-03`. Read either table as "fraction of the way through the run", not as a step number.

The formulas:

```text
step decay      lr = base * gamma ^ floor(t / step_size)
exponential     lr = base * gamma ^ t
linear          lr = base * (1 - (t - W) / (T - W))
cosine          lr = base * 0.5 * (1 + cos(pi * (t - W) / (T - W)))
inverse sqrt    lr = base * sqrt(W / max(t, W))
WSD             lr = base while t < hold, then linear to zero over the last (T - hold) steps
```

Each said out loud, in the order above:

- **Step decay:** "the rate is the base times `gamma` raised to the number of whole intervals you have crossed." `floor` means rounding down to a whole interval, so the rate is flat inside an interval and jumps only at its boundaries. That table's column uses `step_size = 250` and `gamma = 0.5`, so it jumps at 250, 500 and 750: `1.000e-03`, `5.000e-04`, `2.500e-04`, `1.250e-04`, and then holds.
- **Exponential:** "the rate is the base times `gamma` to the power of the step count" — every single step multiplies by `gamma`, so the shrink is continuous instead of stair-stepped.
- **Linear:** "the rate is the base times the fraction of the anneal you have left." Subtract the progress, done.
- **Cosine:** "the rate is the base times one half plus one half of the cosine of pi times your progress." The `cos` term runs from `+1` at the start of the anneal to `-1` at the end, so the multiplier runs from 1 to 0 — smoothly, and slow at first.
- **Inverse sqrt:** "the rate is the base times the square root of the warmup length divided by the current step." Doubling the step count divides the rate by `sqrt(2)`.
- **WSD:** "full rate until `hold`, then a straight line to zero." Warmup is prepended.

Read the last row of the table. Cosine reaches `3e-9`, effectively zero. Linear reaches `1.1e-6`, also effectively zero. Exponential at `gamma = 0.999` reaches `3.7e-4`, still a third of the base rate, because exponential decay never arrives at zero. Step decay gets to `1.25e-4` and would stay there forever — it hits a <Tip term="plateau">To flatten out at a constant value instead of continuing to fall. The other plateau on this page is the P in `ReduceLROnPlateau`, which waits for the validation loss to stop improving before it shrinks the rate.</Tip>. Inverse sqrt reaches `3.2e-4` and keeps falling slowly.

That is the practical difference. If your schedule needs to end near zero, cosine and linear do it by construction. Exponential and step decay need `gamma` tuned against the total step count, and getting it wrong means you finish training at a rate that is still too high.

The `gamma` sensitivity is worth one number each. In the table above, exponential with `gamma = 0.999` lands at `3.681e-04` on a 1000-step run. In the 10000-step table, exponential with `gamma = 0.9999` — a decay per step ten times gentler — lands at `3.679e-04`, essentially the same place. `0.9999` is the right choice for `T = 10000` and the wrong one for `T = 1000`, where it would leave you at `9.048e-04`, still 90% of peak. Step decay has the same coupling in blunter form: with `step_size = 2500` and `gamma = 0.5` on a 10000-step run you take four halvings and finish at `6.250e-05`; stop the run at 3000 steps and you have had one halving and you finish at `5.000e-04`.

<Callout type="info">
  ### Zero-reaching is a property, not an achievement

Cosine, linear and WSD reach zero because their formulas contain `T` and are constructed to vanish at it. Exponential and step decay contain a per-step multiplier and asymptotically approach zero, which is not the same thing. So the honest question when you pick a family is not "does it decay fast enough" but "does it know where the end of the run is". If it does not, tune `gamma` to `T` or you will finish at whatever the curve happens to be at, and the annealing benefit of the last tenth of a percent never materializes.

</Callout>

## Cosine annealing

The failure this shape prevents is the flat late-training stretch where the loss stops improving: the run still moving at close to the peak rate when it is already sitting on a minimum, so the loss curve plateaus without the parameters ever coming to rest. Cosine's answer is to spend the last quarter of the run falling off a cliff.

The most widely used schedule, and the reason is the shape:

```text
lr(t) = eta_min + 0.5 * (base - eta_min) * (1 + cos(pi * t / T))
```

Read out loud: "start at the floor `eta_min`, then add half the distance between the floor and the peak, times one plus a cosine of pi times your progress through the run." At progress 0 the cosine is `1`, so the added term is the full `base - eta_min` and you are at the peak. At progress 1 the cosine is `-1` and you are at the floor. At progress 0.5 the cosine is `0` and you are exactly halfway.

Fractions of the base rate at each point, with `eta_min = 0` and `T = 1000`:

| `t` | Fraction of base |
| :-- | :--------------- |
| 0   | `1.0000`         |
| 250 | `0.8536`         |
| 500 | `0.5000`         |
| 750 | `0.1464`         |
| 999 | `0.0000`         |

It holds near the base rate for the first quarter, passes through half at the midpoint, and drops steeply through the last quarter. Compare that with linear decay, which is at `0.75` at `t = 250` and `0.25` at `t = 750`. Cosine spends more of the run at a high rate and does its annealing faster at the end, which is the shape that works best empirically.

The precise version of that comparison: cosine is above linear for the first half of the anneal and below it for the second half, and the two have identical averages (`0.5001` of peak over the 10000-step run measured above). The difference is entirely about _where_ the slow part sits. Linear gives you a gentle, constant reduction that starts the moment warmup ends; cosine defers the reduction and then delivers it hard.

The steep final drop is doing real work. A fast anneal at the end lets the parameters settle into a sharp minimum they were orbiting, which is the mechanism SGDR exploits with a <Tip term="restart">Resetting the learning rate back to the peak partway through a run instead of letting it keep decaying. The moment the rate snaps back up is the end of one cycle and the start of the next.</Tip>.

## Noam and inverse sqrt

The failure this shape prevents is having to know `T`: warmup and decay come out of one expression, so the curve is defined for an unbounded run. It also bakes in a rate that shrinks automatically as the model gets wider.

The original Transformer used a schedule that combines linear warmup with inverse-square-root decay in a single expression:

```text
lr(step) = base_factor * d_model^(-0.5) * min( step^(-0.5),  step * warmup^(-1.5) )
```

Read it as: "take the smaller of two curves." One of them, `step * warmup^(-1.5)`, grows with the step count and is what you use early; the other, `step^(-0.5)`, falls with it and is what you use later. `min` picks the growing one while you are below the warmup length and the falling one afterwards, so the peak is not a special case in the code — it is where the two curves cross. The `d_model^(-0.5)` in front has nothing to do with time: it says a wider model needs a smaller rate.

The `min` picks warmup while `step < warmup` and decay afterwards, so there is no branch. With `d_model = 512` and `warmup = 4000`:

| Step   | Learning rate | Phase                 |
| :----- | :------------ | :-------------------- |
| 1      | `1.747e-07`   | warmup, linear        |
| 100    | `1.747e-05`   | warmup, linear        |
| 1000   | `1.747e-04`   | warmup, linear        |
| 3999   | `6.986e-04`   | warmup, linear        |
| 4000   | `6.988e-04`   | peak                  |
| 4001   | `6.987e-04`   | decay, `1/sqrt(step)` |
| 8000   | `4.941e-04`   | decay                 |
| 50000  | `1.976e-04`   | decay                 |
| 200000 | `9.882e-05`   | decay                 |

The peak is `d_model^(-0.5) * warmup^(-0.5) = 6.987712e-04`, reached exactly at `step = warmup`. Doubling the warmup steps lowers the peak by `sqrt(2)` and moves it later. Doubling `d_model` lowers it by `sqrt(2)` as well, which is the part of the formula that scales the rate down for wider models.

Both halves of that claim are checkable: `512^(-0.5) = 0.0441942` and `4000^(-0.5) = 0.0158114`, whose product is `6.987712e-04`, exactly the table's peak. Swapping `warmup` to 8000 gives `512^(-0.5) * 8000^(-0.5) = 4.941059e-04`, and the ratio of the two peaks is `1.414214`, which is `sqrt(2)`.

The `1/sqrt(step)` tail never approaches zero. At 200000 steps the rate is still `9.9e-5`, a seventh of the peak. That is fine for the original Transformer, which was trained to a fixed step count with a large model, and it is the reason modern LLM pretraining moved to cosine or WSD, which do reach zero.

Note that Noam's shape and the "inverse sqrt" column of the decay tables are the same curve: a linear ramp to a peak at step `W`, then `base * sqrt(W / t)`. That column is at `3.162e-04` at step 10000 with `W = 1000`, i.e. `sqrt(1000 / 10000) = 0.3162` of peak. Noam is that shape with the peak chosen by the model width instead of by you.

## Warmup-stable-decay

The failure WSD prevents is committing to a step budget you do not have. Cosine needs `T` before the first step; WSD does not need it until the last.

WSD holds the base rate for most of training and anneals only at the end:

```text
lr(t) = base * (t + 1) / W                          t < W
lr(t) = base                                        W <= t < hold
lr(t) = base * (T - t) / (T - hold)                 t >= hold
```

Read the three lines as three phases, in the order the name gives: warm up, stay flat, then "the rate is the base times the fraction of the decay window you have left." `T - t` counts the steps remaining and `T - hold` is the whole decay window, so the multiplier is 1 at the start of the window and 0 at the end of the run.

The table above uses `W = 100`, `hold = 700`, `T = 1000`, so 70% of the run is at a constant rate and the last 30% decays linearly. Recent large-scale runs use 10% warmup, 80% stable, 10% decay.

The advantage is that you do not have to know `T` in advance. With cosine, deciding to train for twice as many steps changes the rate at every step you have already taken, so you cannot extend a run without restarting it. With WSD you keep going at the base rate and anneal whenever you decide to stop. That makes it the natural choice for runs where the budget is not fixed upfront, and it is why MiniCPM and a growing number of pretraining runs use it.

The measured cost of that freedom is in the averages above: WSD runs at `0.8001` of peak for the whole run, against `0.5001` for cosine, because it refuses to slow down until the last 30%. It also anneals over 3000 steps instead of 9000, so the drop is steeper — from `1.000e-03` to `0.000e+00` in the 10000-step table.

A useful side effect: you can branch off the stable phase and anneal several different endpoints from the same checkpoint, which gives you multiple models for the cost of one long run plus several short anneals.

## SGDR restarts

The failure SGDR targets is a different one: not a bad rate at a bad time, but a good rate that spends the entire run refining the single minimum it first landed in.

Cosine annealing with warm restarts runs the cosine curve repeatedly, each <Tip term="cycle">One complete pass of a decay curve from the peak down to the floor. In SGDR the schedule is a string of cycles rather than one long fall.</Tip> longer than the last:

```text
lr(t) = eta_min + 0.5 * (base - eta_min) * (1 + cos(pi * t_cur / T_i))
```

where `T_i` is the length of the current cycle and `t_cur` is the position within it. Read it as: the ordinary cosine formula with `t` replaced by `t_cur`, so the curve is always measured from the start of the cycle you happen to be in. That one substitution is the entire change. When `t_cur` reaches `T_i` the cosine comes back to `+1`, the rate snaps to the peak, and the count restarts.

With `T_0 = 10` and `T_mult = 2`, cycles run 10, 20, 40, 80 epochs and restart at epochs 10, 30, 70, 150:

| Epoch | Learning rate | Current `T_i` |
| :---- | :------------ | :------------ |
| 0     | `1.000e-03`   | 10            |
| 5     | `5.000e-04`   | 10            |
| 9     | `2.447e-05`   | 10            |
| 10    | `1.000e-03`   | 20            |
| 15    | `8.536e-04`   | 20            |
| 29    | `6.156e-06`   | 20            |
| 30    | `1.000e-03`   | 40            |
| 50    | `5.000e-04`   | 40            |
| 69    | `1.541e-06`   | 40            |
| 70    | `1.000e-03`   | 80            |
| 109   | `5.196e-04`   | 80            |

The restart epochs are just the cumulative cycle lengths: `10`, `10 + 20 = 30`, `30 + 40 = 70`, `70 + 80 = 150`. And the pattern repeats inside each cycle: epoch 5 is the midpoint of a 10-epoch cycle and reads `5.000e-04`; epoch 50 is the midpoint of a 40-epoch cycle and reads the same `5.000e-04`. Half the peak at half a cycle, every cycle.

Each restart snaps the rate back to base. The idea is that the jump kicks the parameters out of whichever minimum they settled into, and the longer cycle that follows lets them find a wider one. Wide minima generalize better, and the doubling cycle length spends progressively more time in each.

SGDR pairs with <Tip term="snapshot ensembling">Keeping a checkpoint at the bottom of each cycle instead of only at the end of training, then averaging the checkpoints' predictions at inference. Several models for the price of one run.</Tip>: save the weights at the end of each cycle, when the rate is lowest and the model is best, then average the predictions of all the snapshots. That gets you an ensemble for the cost of one training run.

In practice plain cosine without restarts usually matches SGDR on final accuracy and is simpler to reason about, which is why restarts are not common in large-scale training.

## Implementing it

`LambdaLR` covers every schedule above, because all of them are a function of the step count multiplied by the base rate:

```python title="schedule.py"
import math
import torch

def make_schedule(optimizer, total_steps, warmup_steps, hold_frac=None):
    def lr_lambda(step):
        if step < warmup_steps:
            return (step + 1) / warmup_steps
        if hold_frac is not None:
            hold = int(total_steps * hold_frac)
            if step < hold:
                return 1.0
            return max(0.0, (total_steps - step) / (total_steps - hold))
        progress = (step - warmup_steps) / max(1, total_steps - warmup_steps)
        return 0.5 * (1 + math.cos(math.pi * progress))

    return torch.optim.lr_scheduler.LambdaLR(optimizer, lr_lambda)
```

The function returns a multiplier, not a learning rate. `LambdaLR` multiplies it by the `lr` each parameter group was constructed with, so `return 1.0` means the base rate and `return 0.5` means half of it.

That multiplier is exactly the $m(t)$ of the Start here section, and the three branches of `lr_lambda` are the three lines of the WSD formula: warm up, hold at `1.0`, then anneal linearly. Drop the `hold_frac` branch and you have warmup plus cosine. So `make_schedule` with `hold_frac` set is WSD, and with it left as `None` is warmup+cosine — the two most used families on this page in about ten lines.

Wiring it into the harness from [Training Loop](/ai/pytorch/training-loop):

```python title="with_scheduler.py"
optimizer = torch.optim.AdamW(model.parameters(), lr=3e-4, weight_decay=0.1)
scheduler = make_schedule(optimizer, total_steps=len(loader) * epochs, warmup_steps=500)

for epoch in range(epochs):
    for x, y in loader:
        optimizer.zero_grad()
        loss = loss_fn(model(x), y)
        loss.backward()
        torch.nn.utils.clip_grad_norm_(model.parameters(), 1.0)
        optimizer.step()
        scheduler.step()          # per step, not per epoch
```

The unit the schedule counts in — <Tip term="steps versus epochs">A step (or iteration) is one batch: one forward pass, one backward pass, one update. An epoch is one full pass over the training set, which is `len(loader)` steps.</Tip> — is the single most common way to break a schedule: `total_steps` has to be in the same unit as whatever calls `scheduler.step()`.

<Callout type="warn">
  ### `scheduler.step()` per step or per epoch

Both exist and they are not interchangeable. `CosineAnnealingLR` and `LambdaLR` are usually stepped
per epoch, in which case `total_steps` means epochs. Transformer training steps per iteration, in
which case `total_steps` means iterations. If you pass an iteration count to a scheduler you step
per epoch, the run finishes at 1% of the way through the curve and never anneals. Print
`scheduler.get_last_lr()` at the start and end of training to check.

</Callout>

The built-in alternatives, all stepped per epoch by default:

```python
torch.optim.lr_scheduler.StepLR(opt, step_size=30, gamma=0.1)
torch.optim.lr_scheduler.MultiStepLR(opt, milestones=[30, 60, 80], gamma=0.1)
torch.optim.lr_scheduler.ExponentialLR(opt, gamma=0.999)
torch.optim.lr_scheduler.CosineAnnealingLR(opt, T_max=100, eta_min=0)
torch.optim.lr_scheduler.CosineAnnealingWarmRestarts(opt, T_0=10, T_mult=2, eta_min=0)
torch.optim.lr_scheduler.ReduceLROnPlateau(opt, mode="min", factor=0.1, patience=10)
torch.optim.lr_scheduler.SequentialLR(opt, [warmup, cosine], milestones=[500])
```

`SequentialLR` composes warmup and decay without writing a lambda. `ReduceLROnPlateau` is the only one driven by a metric rather than a counter, and it is the right choice when you do not know how long training will take. It calls `scheduler.step(val_loss)` after each validation pass.

Mapping the families on this page to those calls, since the names do not line up with the formulas:

| Family in this page                | Built-in call                                               |
| :--------------------------------- | :---------------------------------------------------------- |
| Step decay                         | `StepLR`, or `MultiStepLR` for a fixed list of milestones   |
| Exponential                        | `ExponentialLR`                                             |
| Cosine annealing                   | `CosineAnnealingLR` with `T_max` equal to the anneal length |
| SGDR restarts                      | `CosineAnnealingWarmRestarts` with `T_0` and `T_mult`       |
| Warmup plus any decay              | `SequentialLR`, or `LambdaLR` with the `lr_lambda` above    |
| Inverse sqrt / Noam                | `LambdaLR` only                                             |
| WSD                                | `LambdaLR` only (`hold_frac` in `make_schedule`)            |
| "Shrink it when validation stalls" | `ReduceLROnPlateau`                                         |

  chart={`

| Situation                            | Schedule                                          |
| :----------------------------------- | :------------------------------------------------ |
| LLM pretraining, fixed token budget  | Warmup + cosine to zero                           |
| LLM pretraining, open-ended          | WSD                                               |
| Reproducing the original Transformer | Noam                                              |
| CNN classification                   | Step decay at 30/60/80 epochs, or cosine          |
| Fine-tuning, a few thousand steps    | Linear warmup over 3% to 10%, then linear to zero |
| Don't know how long it will take     | `ReduceLROnPlateau`                               |
| Want an ensemble for free            | SGDR with snapshot ensembling                     |

The peak rate matters more than the shape. Cosine against linear against WSD is worth a fraction of a percent; getting the base rate wrong by a factor of three is worth ten percent. Tune the base rate first, then pick a shape.

Which is the same conclusion the measured tables point at. At the halfway mark of a 10000-step run the seven families span `2.500e-04` to `1.000e-03`, a factor of four, and their run-long averages sit between `0.4688` and `0.8001` of the peak. A base rate that is wrong by three is wrong by three at every step of every one of those curves.

## Interview questions

<Accordions>
  <Accordion title="Why do transformers need learning rate warmup?">

    Adam's second-moment estimate starts at zero and takes hundreds of steps to become a reliable average, so early steps are large relative to the information the optimizer has. Warmup keeps them small until `v` has accumulated. Post-norm transformers need it more than pre-norm ones.

  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="What is the cosine annealing formula?">

    `eta_min + 0.5 * (base - eta_min) * (1 + cos(pi * t / T))`. At the midpoint it is exactly halfway between base and `eta_min`, and it holds near the base rate for the first quarter.

  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Why does exponential decay not reach zero?">

    It asymptotes. At `gamma = 0.999` over 1000 steps the rate ends at `3.681e-4`, a third of the base. Cosine and linear reach `3e-9` and `1.1e-6` on the same budget.

  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="What advantage does WSD have over cosine?">

    You do not have to know the total step count in advance. Extending a cosine run changes the rate at every step already taken, so you would have to restart. WSD holds at the base rate and anneals whenever you choose to stop.

  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="What is the Noam schedule's peak?">

    `d_model^(-0.5) * warmup^(-0.5)`, reached at `step = warmup`. For `d_model = 512` and `warmup = 4000` that is `6.988e-04`.

  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title={<>What does <code>T_mult</code> do in SGDR?</>}>

    Multiplies the cycle length after each restart. With `T_0 = 10` and `T_mult = 2`, cycles run 10, 20, 40, 80 epochs and restart at epochs 10, 30, 70, 150.

  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="What is snapshot ensembling?">

    Saving weights at the end of each SGDR cycle, when the rate is lowest, and averaging the predictions of all snapshots. An ensemble for the cost of one training run.

  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title={<>Does <code>scheduler.step()</code> go inside or outside the batch loop?</>}>

    It depends on what `total_steps` means. Transformer training steps per iteration. Classic CNN training steps per epoch. Mixing them up means the curve completes in 1% of the run or never completes at all.

  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title={<>What does <code>LambdaLR</code>'s function return?</>}>

    A multiplier on the base `lr` of each parameter group, not a learning rate. `1.0` means the base rate.

  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Which matters more, the base rate or the shape of the decay?">

    The base rate, by a wide margin. The choice between cosine, linear, and WSD is worth a fraction of a percent. A base rate that is off by a factor of three costs far more.

  </Accordion>
</Accordions>
