A **learning rate schedule** is a rule that sets the learning rate at each step of training instead of holding it constant. The [learning rate](/ai/neural-networks/gradient-descent#the-learning-rate-decides-everything) is the most important hyperparameter, and the best value is not the same throughout a run: large steps make fast progress early, but near the end they prevent the parameters from settling into a minimum. Almost every modern training run uses a schedule, and it typically improves final quality noticeably over a constant rate.

**Builds on:** [Gradient Descent](/ai/neural-networks/gradient-descent) and [Optimizers](/ai/neural-networks/optimizers), in particular Adam's running averages.

---

## The two jobs of a schedule

Every schedule can be written as a peak rate times a shape $m(t)$ between 0 and 1:

$$
\eta(t) = \eta_{\text{peak}} \cdot m(t)
$$

The shape has to solve two problems, one at each end of training:

1. **At the start: avoid diverging before the optimizer knows anything.** The parameters are random, gradients are large and erratic, and Adam's second-moment estimate is built from only a few samples. A full-size step on that information can throw the parameters into a bad region from which training never recovers, or produce a loss spike. **Warmup** starts with a tiny learning rate and ramps it up.
2. **At the end: let the parameters settle.** Near a minimum, gradients are small and noisy. A large learning rate makes the parameters bounce around the minimum rather than descend into it, so the loss plateaus above where it could be. **Decay**, or **annealing**, shrinks the rate toward zero so the final steps are small and precise. The loss typically drops noticeably during the decay phase.

So most schedules have the shape small → large → small.

## A schedule computed by hand

Peak rate $10^{-3}$, total $T = 10$ steps, warmup over the first $W = 2$, then cosine decay:

$$
m(t) = \begin{cases} \dfrac{t + 1}{W} & t < W \\[2mm] \tfrac{1}{2}\left(1 + \cos\left(\pi\,\dfrac{t - W}{T - W}\right)\right) & t \ge W \end{cases}
$$

| Step $t$ | Phase  | $m(t)$ | Learning rate        |
| -------: | :----- | -----: | :------------------- |
|        0 | warmup |  0.500 | $5.0 \times 10^{-4}$ |
|        1 | warmup |  1.000 | $1.0 \times 10^{-3}$ |
|        2 | cosine |  1.000 | $1.0 \times 10^{-3}$ |
|        4 | cosine |  0.854 | $8.5 \times 10^{-4}$ |
|        6 | cosine |  0.500 | $5.0 \times 10^{-4}$ |
|        8 | cosine |  0.146 | $1.5 \times 10^{-4}$ |
|        9 | cosine |  0.038 | $3.8 \times 10^{-5}$ |

Two things to notice. Warmup uses $t + 1$ so the first step is not wasted at a rate of 0. And cosine decay is exactly half the peak at the midpoint of the decay ($t = 6$), stays high before it and drops steeply after: half of the total reduction happens in the last quarter of the run.

## Warmup

Linear warmup over the first $W$ steps is nearly universal for transformers and common elsewhere. Warmup matters most for:

- **Adam-family optimizers.** Their second-moment estimate needs many steps to become reliable; until then step sizes are poorly calibrated.
- **Large learning rates and large batches.** The [linear scaling rule](/ai/neural-networks/gradient-descent#epochs-iterations-and-batch-size) for large batches only works with warmup.
- **Post-norm transformers**, which diverge at initialization without it (see [Normalization](/ai/neural-networks/normalization#where-normalization-goes-pre-norm-and-post-norm)).

Typical lengths:

| Model                           | Warmup                     |
| :------------------------------ | :------------------------- |
| Original Transformer            | 4,000 steps                |
| BERT                            | 10% of training            |
| GPT-3, LLaMA                    | About 2,000 steps or fewer |
| ResNet on ImageNet, large batch | 5 epochs                   |
| Fine-tuning (including LoRA)    | 3–10% of steps             |

The rule of thumb is 1% to 10% of the total steps.

## Decay shapes

The main families, all with peak $10^{-3}$ over 10,000 steps (the warmup-based ones using 1,000 warmup steps):

|   Step | Warmup + cosine      | Step decay (halve every 2,500) | Exponential          | Warmup + linear to 0 | Inverse sqrt         |
| -----: | :------------------- | :----------------------------- | :------------------- | :------------------- | :------------------- |
|      0 | $1 \times 10^{-6}$   | $1.0 \times 10^{-3}$           | $1.0 \times 10^{-3}$ | $1 \times 10^{-6}$   | $1 \times 10^{-6}$   |
|  1,000 | $1.0 \times 10^{-3}$ | $1.0 \times 10^{-3}$           | $9.0 \times 10^{-4}$ | $1.0 \times 10^{-3}$ | $1.0 \times 10^{-3}$ |
|  5,000 | $5.9 \times 10^{-4}$ | $2.5 \times 10^{-4}$           | $6.1 \times 10^{-4}$ | $5.6 \times 10^{-4}$ | $4.5 \times 10^{-4}$ |
| 10,000 | 0                    | $6.3 \times 10^{-5}$           | $3.7 \times 10^{-4}$ | 0                    | $3.2 \times 10^{-4}$ |

- **Step decay** multiplies the rate by a factor (often 0.1) at fixed milestones, for example epochs 30, 60, and 90 for ResNet on ImageNet. Each drop produces a visible step down in the loss curve. It was the standard for CNNs before cosine.
- **Exponential decay** multiplies by a constant every step. It never reaches zero, so it never fully anneals.
- **Cosine annealing** (Loshchilov and Hutter, 2016) follows half a cosine wave from the peak to zero. It stays near the peak for longer than linear decay, then falls steeply. It has no milestones to tune and is the most common default.
- **Linear decay to zero** is equally simple and common for fine-tuning. Cosine and linear spend the same average learning rate (half the peak); they differ in where the slow part sits.
- **Inverse square root**, $\eta \propto 1/\sqrt{t}$ after warmup, decays quickly at first and then very slowly, never reaching zero. The original Transformer's **Noam schedule** is this shape, with the peak set by the model width: $\eta(t) = d^{-1/2}\min(t^{-1/2},\ t \cdot W^{-3/2})$.

The decays that reach zero (cosine and linear) generally give the best final loss for a fixed budget, because the final steps are fully annealed.

## Warmup-stable-decay

Cosine and linear decay have one practical drawback: the shape depends on the total number of steps $T$, which must be fixed before training starts. Deciding halfway through to train twice as long changes what the learning rate should have been at every earlier step.

**Warmup-stable-decay (WSD)** removes this dependency. It warms up, holds the peak constant for most of training, and decays only at the end — typically 10% warmup, 80% stable, and a final 10% linear (or similar) decay to zero. Training can continue at the stable rate for as long as wanted, and the short decay is run whenever a finished model is needed. Several decay branches can even be run from the same stable-phase checkpoint, giving multiple finished models for the cost of one long run. WSD is increasingly used for language-model pretraining (MiniCPM, among others), and matches cosine's final quality while spending more of the run at the peak rate.

---

## SGDR: warm restarts

**SGDR** (Stochastic Gradient Descent with Warm Restarts; Loshchilov and Hutter, 2016) repeats the cosine decay several times, jumping back to the peak at the start of each cycle, often doubling the cycle length each time (10, 20, 40, … epochs). Each restart can kick the parameters out of one basin into another, and saving a checkpoint at the end of each cycle, where the rate is lowest, gives several different well-converged models from one run — **snapshot ensembling** ([Transfer Learning and Ensembles](/ai/classical-deep-learning/transfer-and-ensembles#bagging)).

## Implementing schedules in PyTorch

A schedule wraps the optimizer and is stepped once per **optimizer step** (not per epoch, unless the schedule is defined in epochs):

```python title="schedule.py"
import math
import torch

def warmup_cosine(step, warmup=1000, total=10000):
    if step < warmup:
        return (step + 1) / warmup
    progress = (step - warmup) / (total - warmup)
    return 0.5 * (1 + math.cos(math.pi * progress))

optimizer = torch.optim.AdamW(model.parameters(), lr=1e-3, weight_decay=0.1)
scheduler = torch.optim.lr_scheduler.LambdaLR(optimizer, lr_lambda=warmup_cosine)

for x, y in loader:
    loss = loss_fn(model(x), y)
    optimizer.zero_grad(); loss.backward(); optimizer.step()
    scheduler.step()                      # after optimizer.step(), once per step
```

`LambdaLR` multiplies the optimizer's base learning rate by whatever the function returns, so any shape can be expressed. Built-ins cover the common cases: `StepLR` and `MultiStepLR`, `ExponentialLR`, `CosineAnnealingLR`, `CosineAnnealingWarmRestarts` (SGDR), `SequentialLR` to chain a warmup and a decay, `OneCycleLR` (warmup then anneal in one call), and `ReduceLROnPlateau`, which cuts the rate when validation loss stops improving.

The most common bug is a mismatch between the units of the schedule and how often `scheduler.step()` is called: a cosine schedule defined over 100 "steps" but stepped once per batch finishes its decay in the first fraction of the first epoch.

## Choosing a schedule

| Situation                                  | Schedule                                            |
| :----------------------------------------- | :-------------------------------------------------- |
| Transformer or LLM with a fixed budget     | Linear warmup + cosine decay to zero (or near zero) |
| Pretraining with an open-ended budget      | Warmup-stable-decay                                 |
| CNN image classification                   | Warmup + cosine, or step decay at fixed epochs      |
| Fine-tuning for a few thousand steps       | Warmup over 3–10%, then linear decay to zero        |
| Unknown training length, small experiments | `ReduceLROnPlateau` on validation loss              |

**The peak rate matters far more than the shape.** Switching between cosine, linear, and WSD typically changes final quality by a fraction of a percent; a peak learning rate off by a factor of three can cost far more. Tune the peak first (see [Hyperparameter Search](/ai/neural-networks/hyperparameter-search)), then pick a standard shape.

## Key terms

| Term                           | Meaning                                                              |
| :----------------------------- | :------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **learning rate schedule**     | A rule giving the learning rate at each step                         |
| **warmup**                     | Ramping the rate up from near zero over the first steps              |
| **decay / annealing**          | Shrinking the rate toward zero so training can settle into a minimum |
| **step decay**                 | Multiplying the rate by a constant factor at fixed milestones        |
| **cosine annealing**           | Decaying along half a cosine wave from the peak to zero              |
| **inverse square root / Noam** | Decay proportional to $1/\sqrt{t}$ after warmup                      |
| **WSD**                        | Warmup, a long constant phase, and a short final decay               |
| **SGDR**                       | Repeated cosine cycles with restarts to the peak rate                |

## Interview questions

<Accordions>
  <Accordion title="Why not use a constant learning rate?">

    The best step size changes during training. Early on, the optimizer's statistics are unreliable and large steps risk divergence, so the rate should start small. In the middle, large steps make fast progress. Near the end, gradients are small and noisy, so a large rate makes the parameters bounce around the minimum; decaying it lets them settle, which usually lowers the final loss noticeably.

  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Why does Adam in particular benefit from warmup?">

    Adam divides by the square root of a running average of squared gradients. In the first steps that average comes from only a few noisy samples, so the normalization is unreliable and step sizes can be badly miscalibrated just when the parameters are random and gradients are largest. Warmup keeps the steps small until the statistics have settled.

  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="What is the learning rate at the midpoint of a cosine decay?">

    Exactly half the peak: the multiplier is $\tfrac{1}{2}(1 + \cos(\pi/2)) = 0.5$. Cosine stays above half the peak for the first half of the decay and falls steeply in the second, with half the total reduction happening in the final quarter.

  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="What problem does warmup-stable-decay solve?">

    Cosine and linear decays depend on the total number of steps, which must be fixed before training starts; extending a run changes the ideal rate for every step already taken. WSD holds the peak rate constant for most of training and decays only at the end, so training can be extended indefinitely and annealed whenever a finished model is needed, even from several branches of the same checkpoint.

  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="What is the most common bug when implementing a schedule?">

    Calling `scheduler.step()` at a different frequency from the one the schedule was defined in — per batch when the schedule counts epochs, or vice versa — so it decays far too fast or too slowly. Calling it before `optimizer.step()` also shifts the schedule by one step and triggers a warning in PyTorch.

  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Which matters more: the schedule's shape or its peak learning rate?">

    The peak. Differences between cosine, linear, and WSD shapes are usually worth a fraction of a percent, while a peak rate off by a factor of three can cost much more or cause divergence. The peak should be tuned first and a standard shape chosen afterwards.

  </Accordion>
</Accordions>
