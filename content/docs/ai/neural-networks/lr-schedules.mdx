The learning rate is the one hyperparameter that has to change during a run. A constant value is either too large at the start, before the optimizer has any idea which direction is downhill, or too large at the end, when it overshoots a minimum it has already found. Schedules exist to be small at both ends and large in the middle.

Six families cover everything in use. This page gives the formula, the measured curve, and the situation each one belongs to.

---

## Why the rate has to move

Two separate problems, solved by two separate halves of the schedule.

**Early in training** the parameters are random and the gradient points in a direction that is only loosely related to where the minimum is. Adam compounds this, because its second-moment estimate `v` starts at zero and takes hundreds of steps to become a reliable average. Taking full-size steps on that information throws the parameters somewhere worse. Warmup exists to keep the early steps small until the optimizer has statistics worth trusting.

**Late in training** the parameters are near a minimum and the gradient is small but noisy. A large learning rate makes the parameters orbit the minimum rather than settle into it. Decay shrinks the steps so the final position is close to the best one the run visited. This is worth a surprising amount: on most tasks, annealing to zero recovers one to three tenths of a percent of accuracy over stopping at the peak rate.

---

## Warmup

Linear warmup ramps the rate from zero to the base value over the first `W` steps:

```text
lr(t) = base * (t + 1) / W        for t < W
```

Using `t + 1` rather than `t` means step 0 gets a nonzero rate. With plain `t / W` the first step does nothing at all, which wastes it.

Typical warmup lengths:

| Model                 | Warmup                |
| :-------------------- | :-------------------- |
| Transformer, original | 4000 steps            |
| BERT base             | 10% of training steps |
| GPT-2, GPT-3          | 375 to 2000 steps     |
| LLaMA                 | 2000 steps            |
| CNN on ImageNet       | 5 epochs              |
| LoRA fine-tune        | 3% to 10% of steps    |

The rule of thumb is 1% to 10% of total steps. Warmup matters more for large learning rates, for Adam-family optimizers, and for models with normalization layers whose running statistics have not settled yet.

## The decay families

All six, measured at base `1e-3`, total `1000` steps, warmup `100`:

| Step `t` | Warmup + cosine | Step decay  | Exponential | Linear to zero | Inverse sqrt | WSD         |
| :------- | :-------------- | :---------- | :---------- | :------------- | :----------- | :---------- |
| 0        | `1.000e-05`     | `1.000e-03` | `1.000e-03` | `1.000e-05`    | `1.000e-05`  | `1.000e-05` |
| 1        | `2.000e-05`     | `1.000e-03` | `9.990e-04` | `2.000e-05`    | `2.000e-05`  | `2.000e-05` |
| 50       | `5.100e-04`     | `1.000e-03` | `9.512e-04` | `5.100e-04`    | `5.100e-04`  | `5.100e-04` |
| 100      | `1.000e-03`     | `1.000e-03` | `9.048e-04` | `1.000e-03`    | `1.000e-03`  | `1.000e-03` |
| 250      | `9.330e-04`     | `5.000e-04` | `7.787e-04` | `8.333e-04`    | `6.325e-04`  | `1.000e-03` |
| 500      | `5.868e-04`     | `2.500e-04` | `6.064e-04` | `5.556e-04`    | `4.472e-04`  | `1.000e-03` |
| 750      | `1.786e-04`     | `1.250e-04` | `4.722e-04` | `2.778e-04`    | `3.651e-04`  | `8.333e-04` |
| 999      | `3.046e-09`     | `1.250e-04` | `3.681e-04` | `1.111e-06`    | `3.164e-04`  | `3.333e-06` |

The formulas:

```text
step decay      lr = base * gamma ^ floor(t / step_size)
exponential     lr = base * gamma ^ t
linear          lr = base * (1 - (t - W) / (T - W))
cosine          lr = base * 0.5 * (1 + cos(pi * (t - W) / (T - W)))
inverse sqrt    lr = base * sqrt(W / max(t, W))
WSD             lr = base while t < hold, then linear to zero over the last (T - hold) steps
```

Read the last row of the table. Cosine reaches `3e-9`, effectively zero. Linear reaches `1.1e-6`, also effectively zero. Exponential at `gamma = 0.999` reaches `3.7e-4`, still a third of the base rate, because exponential decay never arrives at zero. Step decay plateaus at `1.25e-4` and would stay there forever. Inverse sqrt reaches `3.2e-4` and keeps falling slowly.

That is the practical difference. If your schedule needs to end near zero, cosine and linear do it by construction. Exponential and step decay need `gamma` tuned against the total step count, and getting it wrong means you finish training at a rate that is still too high.

## Cosine annealing

The most widely used schedule, and the reason is the shape:

```text
lr(t) = eta_min + 0.5 * (base - eta_min) * (1 + cos(pi * t / T))
```

Fractions of the base rate at each point, with `eta_min = 0` and `T = 1000`:

| `t` | Fraction of base |
| :-- | :--------------- |
| 0   | `1.0000`         |
| 250 | `0.8536`         |
| 500 | `0.5000`         |
| 750 | `0.1464`         |
| 999 | `0.0000`         |

It holds near the base rate for the first quarter, passes through half at the midpoint, and drops steeply through the last quarter. Compare that with linear decay, which is at `0.75` at `t = 250` and `0.25` at `t = 750`. Cosine spends more of the run at a high rate and does its annealing faster at the end, which is the shape that works best empirically.

The steep final drop is doing real work. A fast anneal at the end lets the parameters settle into a sharp minimum they were orbiting, which is the mechanism SGDR exploits by restarting.

## Noam and inverse sqrt

The original Transformer used a schedule that combines linear warmup with inverse-square-root decay in a single expression:

```text
lr(step) = base_factor * d_model^(-0.5) * min( step^(-0.5),  step * warmup^(-1.5) )
```

The `min` picks warmup while `step < warmup` and decay afterwards, so there is no branch. With `d_model = 512` and `warmup = 4000`:

| Step   | Learning rate | Phase                 |
| :----- | :------------ | :-------------------- |
| 1      | `1.747e-07`   | warmup, linear        |
| 100    | `1.747e-05`   | warmup, linear        |
| 1000   | `1.747e-04`   | warmup, linear        |
| 3999   | `6.986e-04`   | warmup, linear        |
| 4000   | `6.988e-04`   | peak                  |
| 4001   | `6.987e-04`   | decay, `1/sqrt(step)` |
| 8000   | `4.941e-04`   | decay                 |
| 50000  | `1.976e-04`   | decay                 |
| 200000 | `9.882e-05`   | decay                 |

The peak is `d_model^(-0.5) * warmup^(-0.5) = 6.987712e-04`, reached exactly at `step = warmup`. Doubling the warmup steps lowers the peak by `sqrt(2)` and moves it later. Doubling `d_model` lowers it by `sqrt(2)` as well, which is the part of the formula that scales the rate down for wider models.

The `1/sqrt(step)` tail never approaches zero. At 200000 steps the rate is still `9.9e-5`, a seventh of the peak. That is fine for the original Transformer, which was trained to a fixed step count with a large model, and it is the reason modern LLM pretraining moved to cosine or WSD, which do reach zero.

## Warmup-stable-decay

WSD holds the base rate for most of training and anneals only at the end:

```text
lr(t) = base * (t + 1) / W                          t < W
lr(t) = base                                        W <= t < hold
lr(t) = base * (T - t) / (T - hold)                 t >= hold
```

The table above uses `W = 100`, `hold = 700`, `T = 1000`, so 70% of the run is at a constant rate and the last 30% decays linearly. Recent large-scale runs use 10% warmup, 80% stable, 10% decay.

The advantage is that you do not have to know `T` in advance. With cosine, deciding to train for twice as many steps changes the rate at every step you have already taken, so you cannot extend a run without restarting it. With WSD you keep going at the base rate and anneal whenever you decide to stop. That makes it the natural choice for runs where the budget is not fixed upfront, and it is why MiniCPM and a growing number of pretraining runs use it.

A useful side effect: you can branch off the stable phase and anneal several different endpoints from the same checkpoint, which gives you multiple models for the cost of one long run plus several short anneals.

## SGDR restarts

Cosine annealing with warm restarts runs the cosine curve repeatedly, each cycle longer than the last:

```text
lr(t) = eta_min + 0.5 * (base - eta_min) * (1 + cos(pi * t_cur / T_i))
```

where `T_i` is the length of the current cycle and `t_cur` is the position within it. With `T_0 = 10` and `T_mult = 2`, cycles run 10, 20, 40, 80 epochs and restart at epochs 10, 30, 70, 150:

| Epoch | Learning rate | Current `T_i` |
| :---- | :------------ | :------------ |
| 0     | `1.000e-03`   | 10            |
| 5     | `5.000e-04`   | 10            |
| 9     | `2.447e-05`   | 10            |
| 10    | `1.000e-03`   | 20            |
| 15    | `8.536e-04`   | 20            |
| 29    | `6.156e-06`   | 20            |
| 30    | `1.000e-03`   | 40            |
| 50    | `5.000e-04`   | 40            |
| 69    | `1.541e-06`   | 40            |
| 70    | `1.000e-03`   | 80            |
| 109   | `5.196e-04`   | 80            |

Each restart snaps the rate back to base. The idea is that the jump kicks the parameters out of whichever minimum they settled into, and the longer cycle that follows lets them find a wider one. Wide minima generalize better, and the doubling cycle length spends progressively more time in each.

SGDR pairs with snapshot ensembling: save the weights at the end of each cycle, when the rate is lowest and the model is best, then average the predictions of all the snapshots. That gets you an ensemble for the cost of one training run.

In practice plain cosine without restarts usually matches SGDR on final accuracy and is simpler to reason about, which is why restarts are not common in large-scale training.

## Implementing it

`LambdaLR` covers every schedule above, because all of them are a function of the step count multiplied by the base rate:

```python title="schedule.py"
import math
import torch

def make_schedule(optimizer, total_steps, warmup_steps, hold_frac=None):
    def lr_lambda(step):
        if step < warmup_steps:
            return (step + 1) / warmup_steps
        if hold_frac is not None:
            hold = int(total_steps * hold_frac)
            if step < hold:
                return 1.0
            return max(0.0, (total_steps - step) / (total_steps - hold))
        progress = (step - warmup_steps) / max(1, total_steps - warmup_steps)
        return 0.5 * (1 + math.cos(math.pi * progress))

    return torch.optim.lr_scheduler.LambdaLR(optimizer, lr_lambda)
```

The function returns a multiplier, not a learning rate. `LambdaLR` multiplies it by the `lr` each parameter group was constructed with, so `return 1.0` means the base rate and `return 0.5` means half of it.

Wiring it into the harness from [Training Loop](/ai/pytorch/training-loop):

```python title="with_scheduler.py"
optimizer = torch.optim.AdamW(model.parameters(), lr=3e-4, weight_decay=0.1)
scheduler = make_schedule(optimizer, total_steps=len(loader) * epochs, warmup_steps=500)

for epoch in range(epochs):
    for x, y in loader:
        optimizer.zero_grad()
        loss = loss_fn(model(x), y)
        loss.backward()
        torch.nn.utils.clip_grad_norm_(model.parameters(), 1.0)
        optimizer.step()
        scheduler.step()          # per step, not per epoch
```

<Callout type="warn">
  ### `scheduler.step()` per step or per epoch

Both exist and they are not interchangeable. `CosineAnnealingLR` and `LambdaLR` are usually stepped
per epoch, in which case `total_steps` means epochs. Transformer training steps per iteration, in
which case `total_steps` means iterations. If you pass an iteration count to a scheduler you step
per epoch, the run finishes at 1% of the way through the curve and never anneals. Print
`scheduler.get_last_lr()` at the start and end of training to check.

</Callout>

The built-in alternatives, all stepped per epoch by default:

```python
torch.optim.lr_scheduler.StepLR(opt, step_size=30, gamma=0.1)
torch.optim.lr_scheduler.MultiStepLR(opt, milestones=[30, 60, 80], gamma=0.1)
torch.optim.lr_scheduler.ExponentialLR(opt, gamma=0.999)
torch.optim.lr_scheduler.CosineAnnealingLR(opt, T_max=100, eta_min=0)
torch.optim.lr_scheduler.CosineAnnealingWarmRestarts(opt, T_0=10, T_mult=2, eta_min=0)
torch.optim.lr_scheduler.ReduceLROnPlateau(opt, mode="min", factor=0.1, patience=10)
torch.optim.lr_scheduler.SequentialLR(opt, [warmup, cosine], milestones=[500])
```

`SequentialLR` composes warmup and decay without writing a lambda. `ReduceLROnPlateau` is the only one driven by a metric rather than a counter, and it is the right choice when you do not know how long training will take. It calls `scheduler.step(val_loss)` after each validation pass.

| Situation                            | Schedule                                          |
| :----------------------------------- | :------------------------------------------------ |
| LLM pretraining, fixed token budget  | Warmup + cosine to zero                           |
| LLM pretraining, open-ended          | WSD                                               |
| Reproducing the original Transformer | Noam                                              |
| CNN classification                   | Step decay at 30/60/80 epochs, or cosine          |
| Fine-tuning, a few thousand steps    | Linear warmup over 3% to 10%, then linear to zero |
| Don't know how long it will take     | `ReduceLROnPlateau`                               |
| Want an ensemble for free            | SGDR with snapshot ensembling                     |

The peak rate matters more than the shape. Cosine against linear against WSD is worth a fraction of a percent; getting the base rate wrong by a factor of three is worth ten percent. Tune the base rate first, then pick a shape.

## Interview questions

<Accordions>
  <Accordion title="Why do transformers need learning rate warmup?">

    Adam's second-moment estimate starts at zero and takes hundreds of steps to become a reliable average, so early steps are large relative to the information the optimizer has. Warmup keeps them small until `v` has accumulated. Post-norm transformers need it more than pre-norm ones.

  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="What is the cosine annealing formula?">

    `eta_min + 0.5 * (base - eta_min) * (1 + cos(pi * t / T))`. At the midpoint it is exactly halfway between base and `eta_min`, and it holds near the base rate for the first quarter.

  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Why does exponential decay not reach zero?">

    It asymptotes. At `gamma = 0.999` over 1000 steps the rate ends at `3.681e-4`, a third of the base. Cosine and linear reach `3e-9` and `1.1e-6` on the same budget.

  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="What advantage does WSD have over cosine?">

    You do not have to know the total step count in advance. Extending a cosine run changes the rate at every step already taken, so you would have to restart. WSD holds at the base rate and anneals whenever you choose to stop.

  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="What is the Noam schedule's peak?">

    `d_model^(-0.5) * warmup^(-0.5)`, reached at `step = warmup`. For `d_model = 512` and `warmup = 4000` that is `6.988e-04`.

  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title={<>What does <code>T_mult</code> do in SGDR?</>}>

    Multiplies the cycle length after each restart. With `T_0 = 10` and `T_mult = 2`, cycles run 10, 20, 40, 80 epochs and restart at epochs 10, 30, 70, 150.

  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="What is snapshot ensembling?">

    Saving weights at the end of each SGDR cycle, when the rate is lowest, and averaging the predictions of all snapshots. An ensemble for the cost of one training run.

  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title={<>Does <code>scheduler.step()</code> go inside or outside the batch loop?</>}>

    It depends on what `total_steps` means. Transformer training steps per iteration. Classic CNN training steps per epoch. Mixing them up means the curve completes in 1% of the run or never completes at all.

  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title={<>What does <code>LambdaLR</code>'s function return?</>}>

    A multiplier on the base `lr` of each parameter group, not a learning rate. `1.0` means the base rate.

  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Which matters more, the base rate or the shape of the decay?">

    The base rate, by a wide margin. The choice between cosine, linear, and WSD is worth a fraction of a percent. A base rate that is off by a factor of three costs far more.

  </Accordion>
</Accordions>
