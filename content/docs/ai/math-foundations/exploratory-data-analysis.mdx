**EDA** is looking at data before modelling it: plotting each variable, then pairs of variables, to find its shape, its problems, and its surprises. The name and the practice come from John Tukey's 1977 book _Exploratory Data Analysis_, which also introduced the box plot.

This page uses the quartiles from [Descriptive Statistics](/ai/math-foundations/descriptive-statistics) and the distribution shapes from [Probability and Distributions](/ai/math-foundations/probability-and-distributions).

The page covers, in order:

1. **The four plots**
2. **Covariance and correlation**

## The four plots

| Plot             | Shows                                               | Look for                                                 |
| :--------------- | :-------------------------------------------------- | :------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Histogram**    | One numeric variable: bars counting values per bin  | Shape, skew, several peaks, gaps, impossible values      |
| **Box plot**     | One variable's five-number summary                  | Median, spread, and points beyond the whiskers           |
| **Scatter plot** | Two numeric variables, one dot per observation      | Trends, clusters, curved relations, points off the cloud |
| **Density plot** | A smoothed histogram (kernel density estimate, KDE) | Shape without bin artifacts; comparing groups overlaid   |

**Histogram.** The bin width changes the picture: too wide hides structure, too narrow shows noise. Try more than one. Two peaks (bimodal) usually means two groups mixed together, such as weekday and weekend traffic.

**Box plot.** The box runs from Q1 to Q3, with a line at the median. The **whiskers** extend to the most extreme values still within `1.5 * IQR` of the box. Points beyond the whiskers are drawn individually as possible outliers. This is exactly the IQR rule from [Statistical Outlier Detection](/ai/math-foundations/statistical-outlier-detection#iqr-rule), drawn as a picture.

```text
           Q1   median   Q3
   |--------[=====|=======]-----------|        o      o
  min within                     max within   points beyond
  the fence                      the fence    Q3 + 1.5 * IQR
```

**Scatter plot.** The only one of the four that shows how two variables relate. A point can look ordinary on each axis separately and still sit far from the cloud. That is a **multivariate outlier**, and the reason a single-variable rule cannot find it.

**Density plot.** A **kernel density estimate** places a small bump (usually a normal curve) on every data point and adds them up. The bump width (bandwidth) plays the role of the histogram's bin width.

## Covariance and correlation

**Covariance** measures whether two variables move together:

$$
\operatorname{cov}(x, y) = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y})}{n - 1}
$$

When both are above their means together, or below together, the products are positive and covariance is positive. When one is high while the other is low, it is negative. Near 0 means no **linear** relation. The problem is the unit: covariance is in x's unit times y's unit, so its size cannot be read.

**Correlation** (Pearson's `r`) fixes that by dividing by both standard deviations:

$$
r = \frac{\operatorname{cov}(x, y)}{s_x \cdot s_y} \quad (-1 \le r \le +1)
$$

`+1` is a perfect rising straight line, `-1` a perfect falling one, `0` no linear relation. Changing units cannot change it:

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
height_cm = rng.normal(170, 10, size=1_000)
weight_kg = 0.9 * height_cm - 85 + rng.normal(0, 7, size=1_000)
height_m = height_cm / 100
print(f"cov(cm, kg)={np.cov(height_cm, weight_kg)[0, 1]:.2f}  "
      f"cov(m, kg)={np.cov(height_m, weight_kg)[0, 1]:.3f}")
print(f"corr(cm, kg)={np.corrcoef(height_cm, weight_kg)[0, 1]:.3f}  "
      f"corr(m, kg)={np.corrcoef(height_m, weight_kg)[0, 1]:.3f}")

x = np.linspace(-3, 3, 101)
print(f"corr(x, x**2)={np.corrcoef(x, x**2)[0, 1]:.3f}")
```

```text
cov(cm, kg)=90.07  cov(m, kg)=0.901
corr(cm, kg)=0.790  corr(m, kg)=0.790
corr(x, x**2)=-0.000
```

Measuring height in metres instead of centimetres divides the covariance by 100, but the correlation is identical. The last line is the classic trap: `y = x^2` is completely determined by `x`, yet the correlation is 0, because the relation is a curve, not a line. **Correlation 0 means no linear relation, not no relation.** Always look at the scatter plot.

Three more points interviewers check:

- **Correlation is not causation.** Ice cream sales and drownings are correlated because both rise in summer. A third variable (a **confounder**) drives both.
- **Spearman's rank correlation** is Pearson's `r` computed on the ranks of the values instead of the values. It catches any monotonic relation (always rising, even if curved) and resists extreme values.
- **A covariance matrix** holds the covariance of every pair of features. Its diagonal holds each feature's variance. The [Mahalanobis distance](/ai/math-foundations/statistical-outlier-detection#mahalanobis-distance) and PCA (see [Vectors and Matrices](/ai/math-foundations/vectors-and-matrices)) are both built on it.

  <Accordion title="How do you read a box plot, and what are the whiskers?">

    The box runs from Q1 to Q3 with a line at the median, so its height is the IQR. The whiskers reach the most extreme values still within 1.5 times the IQR of the box, and points beyond them are drawn individually as possible outliers. It shows center, spread, skew (an off-center median or uneven whiskers), and outliers in one picture, and is the IQR outlier rule drawn.

  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Covariance versus correlation?">

    Both measure whether two variables move together linearly. Covariance is in the product of the two units, so its size depends on units (metres versus centimetres changed it by 100 times). Correlation divides by both standard deviations, so it is unitless and lies in -1 to 1. Correlation 0 means no linear relation; `y = x^2` has correlation 0 yet is fully determined by `x`.
