Training a model means finding parameters that make a loss small. The only scalable way to do that is to ask the loss which direction increases it, then step the other way. Derivatives answer that question for one variable, gradients answer it for millions.

Symbols on this page are decoded in [Notation and Symbols](/ai/math-foundations/notation-and-symbols).

## The derivative

The derivative of $f$ at $x$ is its instantaneous rate of change, defined as the limit of the average rate over a shrinking interval:

$$
f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x + h) - f(x)}{h}
$$

Piece by piece: $f$ is the function, $x$ is the point you are standing at, and $h$ is a small step sideways. $f(x + h) - f(x)$ is how much the output moved, and dividing by $h$ turns that into "output change per unit of input change". $f'(x)$, pronounced "f prime of x", is the name of the derivative function. $\lim_{h \to 0}$ means: compute it for smaller and smaller $h$ and take the value the results converge to.

The word "instantaneous" is doing real work, because a plain average over an interval is not what you want. For `f(x) = x^2` at `x = 3`, the average slope over a step of size `h` works out to exactly `6 + h`:

| `h`     | average slope over `[3, 3 + h]` |
| :------ | :------------------------------ |
| `1`     | 7.0                             |
| `0.1`   | 6.1                             |
| `0.01`  | 6.01                            |
| `0.001` | 6.001                           |
| `0`     | 6.0, the limit                  |

As `h` shrinks, the interval stops being an average over a stretch of curve and becomes the slope at a single point. That point slope is the slope of the **tangent line**, the straight line that just grazes the curve at `x` and points in the direction the curve is heading there.

Two readings that both matter:

- **Geometric.** `f'(x)` is the slope of the tangent to the curve at `x`.
- **Practical.** `f'(x)` tells you how much `f` changes per unit change in `x`. A derivative of `-3.5` means increasing `x` by a small amount decreases `f` by about 3.5 times that amount.

That second reading is the entire basis of gradient descent. The derivative is not an abstraction, it is a sensitivity coefficient.

```python title="derivative.py"
def numerical_derivative(f, x, h=1e-5):
    return (f(x + h) - f(x - h)) / (2 * h)

f = lambda x: x ** 2
print(numerical_derivative(f, 3.0))   # 6.000000..., matches f'(x) = 2x at x = 3
```

The centered difference `(f(x+h) - f(x-h)) / 2h` is more accurate than the one-sided form because the first-order error terms cancel. You will use this exact formula to verify backpropagation on the last page of this phase.

## Partial derivatives

A function of several variables, like `f(x, y) = x^2 * y + sin(y)`, has one **partial derivative** per variable. Each answers a separate question: if I nudge only `x` and leave everything else alone, how fast does `f` move?

To take the partial with respect to $x$, treat $y$ as a constant and differentiate normally:

$$
f(x, y) = x^2 y + \sin(y)
$$

$$
\frac{\partial f}{\partial x} = 2xy \quad (y \text{ held constant, } \sin(y) \text{ is constant so it vanishes})
$$

$$
\frac{\partial f}{\partial y} = x^2 + \cos(y) \quad (x \text{ held constant, } x^2 \text{ is a coefficient})
$$

"Vanishes" means becomes zero. Nudge $x$ and $\sin(y)$ does not move at all, so that term contributes nothing to $\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}$. Evaluating both partials at $(x=1, y=2)$ gives ordinary arithmetic:

$$
\frac{\partial f}{\partial x} = 2(1)(2) = 4.0
$$

$$
\frac{\partial f}{\partial y} = 1^2 + \cos(2) = 1 - 0.4161 = 0.5839
$$

```python title="partials.py"
import numpy as np

def f(x, y):
    return x ** 2 * y + np.sin(y)

def partial(f, arg_index, point, h=1e-5):
    p = np.array(point, dtype=float)
    plus = p.copy();  plus[arg_index] += h
    minus = p.copy(); minus[arg_index] -= h
    return (f(*plus) - f(*minus)) / (2 * h)

point = (1.0, 2.0)
print(partial(f, 0, point))   # ~4.0,  matches 2xy = 2 * 1 * 2
print(partial(f, 1, point))   # ~0.58, matches x^2 + cos(y) = 1 + cos(2)
```

A neural network loss is a function of every parameter in the model. Each partial derivative says how the loss responds to nudging that one weight, holding all others fixed.

## Differentiation rules

Three rules generate almost every gradient in deep learning:

| Rule              | Operation                                    | Formula                                                       |
| :---------------- | :------------------------------------------- | :------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **Product Rule**  | $\frac{d}{dx}[u(x)v(x)]$                     | $u'(x)v(x) + u(x)v'(x)$                                       |
| **Quotient Rule** | $\frac{d}{dx}\left[\frac{u(x)}{v(x)}\right]$ | $\frac{u'(x)v(x) - u(x)v'(x)}{[v(x)]^2}$                      |
| **Chain Rule**    | $\frac{d}{dx}[g(h(x))]$                      | $g'(h(x)) \cdot h'(x)$ or $\frac{dg}{dh} \cdot \frac{dh}{dx}$ |

The prime notation `'` means "the derivative of", so $u'$ is the derivative of $u$ with respect to $x$.

### Product rule

For $f(x) = u(x)v(x)$:

$$
\frac{d}{dx}[u(x)v(x)] = u'(x)v(x) + u(x)v'(x)
$$

Read it as: derivative of the first times the second, plus the first times derivative of the second. Both factors depend on $x$, so nudging $x$ moves both of them, and each one's contribution gets counted.

Example with $u = x^2$, $v = \sin(x)$:

$$
\frac{d}{dx}[x^2 \sin(x)] = 2x \sin(x) + x^2 \cos(x)
$$

Gated activations use this directly. SwiGLU computes $\text{gate} \odot \text{up}$ where both are learned projections, so its backward pass is a product rule application.

### Quotient rule

For $f(x) = \frac{u(x)}{v(x)}$:

$$
f'(x) = \frac{u'(x)v(x) - u(x)v'(x)}{[v(x)]^2}
$$

Read it as: bottom times derivative of top, minus top times derivative of bottom, all over bottom squared. It is the product rule applied to $u \cdot (1 / v)$, which is why the numerator looks like the product rule with one term flipped.

Example:

$$
\frac{d}{dx}\left[\frac{\sin(x)}{x}\right] = \frac{x\cos(x) - \sin(x)}{x^2}
$$

Softmax differentiation produces a quotient rule, since softmax is $\frac{e^{z_i}}{\sum_j e^{z_j}}$.

### Chain rule

For a composition $f(x) = g(h(x))$, meaning $h$ runs first and $g$ takes its output as input:

$$
f'(x) = g'(h(x)) \cdot h'(x) \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad \frac{df}{dx} = \frac{df}{dh} \cdot \frac{dh}{dx}
$$

Read it as: derivative of the outer function, evaluated at what the inner produced, times the derivative of the inner. Rates of change multiply rather than add, because the inner change is measured in the inner's own units.

Worked example with $g(u) = u^3$ and $h(x) = 2x + 1$:

$$
f(x) = (2x + 1)^3 \implies f'(x) = 3(2x + 1)^2 \cdot 2 = 6(2x + 1)^2
$$

At $x = 1$: $f'(1) = 6 \cdot (3)^2 = 54$.

```python title="chain_rule.py"
x = 1.0
h = 2 * x + 1              # 3.0        forward pass
f = h ** 3                 # 27.0       forward pass

df_dh = 3 * h ** 2         # 27.0       local derivative
dh_dx = 2.0                # 2.0        local derivative
df_dx = df_dh * dh_dx      # 54.0       chain rule
```

Notice the structure of that code: compute the forward values, store intermediates, then multiply local derivatives backwards through the chain. That is backpropagation. Every framework does exactly this, just at scale and automatically.

<Callout type="info">
  The chain rule is why deep networks have vanishing and exploding gradients. A 96-layer network
  multiplies roughly 96 local derivatives together on the way back. If each averages 0.5, the
  product is `0.5^96`, effectively zero. If each averages 2, it overflows. Residual connections and
  normalization layers exist to keep those products near 1.
</Callout>

## The gradient

The **gradient** collects every partial derivative into one vector:

$$
\nabla f(\mathbf{x}) = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_1} \\ \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_2} \\ \vdots \\ \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_n} \end{bmatrix} \quad \text{or for } 2\text{D}\colon \nabla f(x, y) = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{\partial f}{\partial x} \\ \frac{\partial f}{\partial y} \end{bmatrix}
$$

For $n$ inputs it is an $n$-dimensional vector, one entry per input. Back to $f(x, y) = x^2 y + \sin(y)$ at $(1, 2)$, where the partials were 4.0 and 0.5839:

$$
\nabla f(1, 2) = \begin{bmatrix} 4.0 \\ 0.5839 \end{bmatrix}
$$

The first coordinate is about 7 times the second, so at that point $x$ is the axis that moves $f$: nudge $x$ by 0.001 and $f$ rises by roughly 0.004, nudge $y$ by the same amount and it rises by roughly 0.0006.

Two properties make the gradient the core object of optimization:

1. **It points in the direction of steepest ascent.** Moving an infinitesimal step along $\nabla f$ increases $f$ faster than moving in any other direction.
2. **Its magnitude $\|\nabla f\|_2$ is the steepness in that direction.** A large norm means a sharp slope, a near-zero norm means flat terrain.

Therefore $-\nabla f$ points downhill, and gradient descent update is:

$$
\mathbf{w}_{t+1} = \mathbf{w}_t - \eta \nabla f(\mathbf{w}_t)
$$

$\mathbf{w}$ is the parameter vector and $\eta$ (or `lr`) is the learning rate, controlling how far you step. A derivative says which way is down; the learning rate decides how much of that information you act on.

```python title="gradient.py"
def grad_f(point):
    x, y = point
    return np.array([2 * x * y, x ** 2 + np.cos(y)])

w = np.array([1.0, 2.0])
lr = 0.01

for step in range(5):
    g = grad_f(w)
    w = w - lr * g
    print(f"step {step}: w={w.round(4)}  |grad|={np.linalg.norm(g):.4f}")
```

`|grad|` in that output is the gradient's norm, its length.

The gradient norm shrinking toward zero is the signal that you are approaching a stationary point. Monitoring it during training is standard practice, and gradient clipping in Phase 4 caps it to stop exploding values from wrecking a step.

## Directional derivatives

The gradient points uphill, but you often want the rate of change along an arbitrary direction $\mathbf{u}$ (a unit vector, $\|\mathbf{u}\|_2 = 1$). That is the **directional derivative**, written $D_{\mathbf{u}} f$ or $\nabla_{\mathbf{u}} f$:

$$
D_{\mathbf{u}} f = \nabla f(\mathbf{x}) \cdot \mathbf{u} = \|\nabla f(\mathbf{x})\|_2 \|\mathbf{u}\|_2 \cos(\theta) = \|\nabla f(\mathbf{x})\|_2 \cos(\theta)
$$

It is a dot product, which explains the geometric behavior:

| Angle $\theta$ between $\mathbf{u}$ and $\nabla f$ | $D_{\mathbf{u}} f$          | Meaning                           |
| :------------------------------------------------- | :-------------------------- | :-------------------------------- |
| $0^\circ$                                          | $\|\nabla f\|_2$ (Maximum)  | Steepest ascent                   |
| $90^\circ$                                         | $0$                         | Moving tangent to a level contour |
| $180^\circ$                                        | $-\|\nabla f\|_2$ (Minimum) | Steepest descent                  |

Walking straight up the gradient maximizes $\cos(\theta) = 1$. Walking perpendicular to it ($\theta = 90^\circ$) changes nothing at all, which is what "along a level contour" means.

## Jacobians

When a function outputs a **vector** rather than a scalar, one gradient vector is not enough. You need every output's derivative with respect to every input, arranged in a matrix. That is the **Jacobian**.

For a vector-valued function $\mathbf{f}\colon \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m$, the Jacobian matrix $J \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$ contains all first-order partial derivatives:

$$
J = \frac{\partial \mathbf{f}}{\partial \mathbf{x}} = \begin{bmatrix}
\frac{\partial f_1}{\partial x_1} & \frac{\partial f_1}{\partial x_2} & \cdots & \frac{\partial f_1}{\partial x_n} \\[6pt]
\frac{\partial f_2}{\partial x_1} & \frac{\partial f_2}{\partial x_2} & \cdots & \frac{\partial f_2}{\partial x_n} \\[6pt]
\vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\[6pt]
\frac{\partial f_m}{\partial x_1} & \frac{\partial f_m}{\partial x_2} & \cdots & \frac{\partial f_m}{\partial x_n}
\end{bmatrix}
$$

One entry $J_{ij} = \frac{\partial f_i}{\partial x_j}$ per output-input pair. Row $i$ is the transpose of the gradient of output component $f_i$: $\nabla f_i^T$.

```python title="jacobian.py"
def f(v):
    x, y = v
    return np.array([x ** 2 + y, x * y])      # 2 in, 2 out

# analytic Jacobian
def J(v):
    x, y = v
    return np.array([[2 * x, 1.0],     # d(x^2 + y)/dx, d(...)/dy
                     [y,     x]])      # d(x*y)/dx,     d(...)/dy

v = np.array([1.0, 2.0])
print(J(v))    # [[2. 1.]
               #  [2. 1.]]

# numerical check
h = 1e-5
numeric = np.column_stack([(f(v + np.eye(2)[i] * h) - f(v - np.eye(2)[i] * h)) / (2 * h)
                           for i in range(2)])
print(np.allclose(J(v), numeric, atol=1e-5))   # True
```

The two lines of `J(v)` are the two outputs' partials: output 1 is `x^2 + y`, so its derivatives are `2x = 2` and `1`. Output 2 is `x * y`, so `y = 2` and `x = 1`.

A scalar-valued loss over a vector-valued layer is the common case, and the gradient is just a `1 x n` Jacobian.

### Jacobian-vector products

Backpropagation never materializes full Jacobians. Materializing means building the whole object in memory, and for a layer with `n` inputs and `m` outputs, the Jacobian is `m x n`, which for a `4096 x 11008` feed-forward layer is 45 million entries. Instead, frameworks compute a **vector-Jacobian product** (VJP): they take the incoming gradient from downstream (shape `m`) and multiply it by the Jacobian to produce the gradient for the inputs (shape `n`).

```
grad_input = J.T @ grad_output
```

The trick is that the multiplication is all anyone ever needs. Chain rule says the downstream gradient times the local derivative gives the upstream gradient, and the local derivative here is `J`. Computing that product directly costs one matrix multiplication, never a stored `J`, so the memory stays at the size of the vectors instead of the size of the grid between them.

This is the reason reverse-mode autodiff scales to billions of parameters. Phase 3 derives the same identity layer by layer.

## Stationary points: minima, maxima, saddles

A **stationary point** has zero gradient. Three kinds exist, and telling them apart matters because gradient descent stops at all of them.

| Type          | 1D second derivative | Behavior in high dimensions                  |
| :------------ | :------------------- | :------------------------------------------- |
| Local minimum | Positive             | Curves up in every direction                 |
| Local maximum | Negative             | Curves down in every direction               |
| Saddle point  | Mixed signs          | Curves down in some directions, up in others |

`f'(x)` is the slope. `f''(x)` is the derivative of `f'(x)`: how fast the slope itself is changing, which is the <Tip term="curvature">How sharply a curve bends. Positive curvature means the curve opens upward like a cup; negative means it opens downward like a dome.</Tip> of the curve. A stationary point is where the slope is zero, and the sign of the curvature there says whether you are sitting in a bowl or on top of a hill:

```
f(x)  = x^2       f'(x) = 2x      f''(x) = 2      at x=0: slope 0, curvature +2 -> minimum
f(x)  = -x^2      f'(x) = -2x     f''(x) = -2     at x=0: slope 0, curvature -2 -> maximum
```

For many variables, the equivalent test uses the **Hessian**: the matrix of second partial derivatives. Take every partial you already have, differentiate it again with respect to every variable, and arrange the results in an `(n, n)` matrix. For `f(x, y) = x^2 - y^2` that is

```
df/dx = 2x        df/dy = -2y

H = [ d2f/dx2    d2f/dxdy ] = [ 2   0 ]
    [ d2f/dydx   d2f/dy2  ]   [ 0  -2 ]
```

Reading `d2f/dxdy` is "differentiate twice, first with respect to `y`, then with respect to `x`". The two mixed entries usually end up equal, so `H` is symmetric. The eigenvalues of `H` at a stationary point are the curvatures along the directions that the <Tip term="eigenvectors">Directions that a matrix only stretches, never turns. On the vectors-and-matrices page, `A @ v = lambda * v` names exactly those directions.</Tip> name, so `H`'s eigenvalues `2` and `-2` say the point curves up along one axis and down along the other:

```python title="hessian.py"
H = np.array([[2.0, 0.0], [0.0, -2.0]])
print(np.linalg.eigvalsh(H))   # [-2.  2.]  mixed signs -> saddle
```

`f(x, y) = x^2 - y^2` at the origin is the standard saddle, and it is checkable by hand. The gradient is `[2x, -2y]`, which is `[0, 0]` at `(0, 0)`, so it is stationary. Walk along `x` with `y = 0` and `f` rises: `0.0, 0.01, 0.04`. Walk along `y` with `x = 0` and `f` falls: `0.0, -0.01, -0.04`. Same point, zero gradient, opposite verdicts, which is what a pass over a mountain looks like from above: up in one direction, down in the other.

So the rule is: all eigenvalues of `H` positive means a local minimum, all negative means a maximum, mixed signs means a saddle.

<Callout type="warn">
  The popular fear that gradient descent gets trapped in bad local minima is largely misplaced for
  deep networks. Here is the counting argument. A stationary point needs every one of the `n`
  gradient entries to be zero at once, and then it is a local minimum only if all `n` Hessian
  eigenvalues come out positive. If those curvatures were roughly coin flips, the odds of `n`
  agreeing are `0.5^n`: for a million-parameter model that is about `10^-301030`, a number with no
  room left for a real model to hit. (Coins is an idealization; the argument is that the count of
  required agreements grows with `n`, not that each curvature actually flips.) So almost every
  stationary point is a saddle. The practical problem is not a wrong minimum, it is crawling through
  flat saddle regions where the gradient is near zero. Momentum in Adam exists to keep moving when
  the local gradient provides almost no signal.

</Callout>

## Interview questions

<Accordions>
  <Accordion title="Operationally, what does a derivative tell you during training?">

    It is a sensitivity coefficient, not an abstraction. A derivative of `-3.5` means a small increase in that variable decreases the function by about 3.5 times the size of the nudge, which is the entire basis of gradient descent.

  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Why use the centered difference for gradient checking?">

    `(f(x + h) - f(x - h)) / (2 * h)` cancels the first-order error terms, so the estimate is accurate to second order in `h` instead of first. That makes a broken hand-written backward pass show up as a real mismatch rather than as truncation noise.

  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="How do partial derivatives apply to a neural network loss?">

    Take the partial with respect to one parameter by holding every other parameter constant. Each one answers how the loss responds to nudging that single weight, and the stack of them is the gradient.

  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Which differentiation rule explains the backward pass of SwiGLU?">

    The product rule. SwiGLU multiplies two learned projections, `gate * up`, so the gradient of each path needs the other factor: `d/dx [u * v] = u' * v + u * v'`.

  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Why does the softmax backward pass involve the quotient rule?">

    Softmax is a ratio, `exp(zi)` over the sum of `exp(zj)`, and the denominator depends on the same variable as the numerator. Both terms of the quotient rule survive, which is why the Jacobian of softmax is not diagonal.

  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="What is backpropagation, expressed in one sentence about the chain rule?">

    The chain rule evaluated once per node: each local derivative multiplies into the signal flowing backward, so a gradient that is far from the loss is a product of every local derivative between them.

  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="A gradient of exactly zero proves you found a minimum. True or false?">

    False. Stationary points include maxima and saddles, and in high dimensions saddles dominate because the Hessian needs every one of thousands of eigenvalues to be positive. This is why optimizers use momentum and adaptive step sizes instead of stopping at a flat point.

  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Why clip by gradient norm when the norm is already bounded by the largest singular value?">

    The bound is real but huge: a gradient can be scaled by the spectral norm of each layer it passes through, so composing many layers multiplies those bounds together. Clipping rescales the vector without changing its direction, which keeps an exploding batch from throwing the parameters across the space.

  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="What is the difference between a JVP and a VJP, and why do autodiff engines use the VJP?">

    A JVP multiplies a tangent vector forward through the Jacobian, a VJP multiplies a cotangent vector backward through the transpose. Reverse mode costs one backward sweep no matter how many parameters there are, and a training loss is one scalar against millions of weights, so the VJP is the cheap direction.

  </Accordion>
</Accordions>
