Retrieval, attention, and regularization all reduce to two questions: how much do these vectors point the same way, and how large is this vector. The dot product answers the first. Norms answer the second.

Symbols on this page are decoded in [Notation and Symbols](/ai/math-foundations/notation-and-symbols).

## The dot product

For two vectors of equal length, the dot product (also called the **inner product**) multiplies matching entries and sums the results:

$$
\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} a_i b_i = a_1 b_1 + a_2 b_2 + \dots + a_n b_n
$$

Three entries in, three products, one number out. Nothing about the operation mixes positions: entry 1 pairs with entry 1, entry 2 with entry 2. Here it is by hand for `a = [1, 2, 3]` and `b = [4, -5, 6]`:

| Position | `a` says | `b` says | Multiply |
| :------- | :------- | :------- | :------- |
| 1        | 1        | 4        | 4        |
| 2        | 2        | -5       | -10      |
| 3        | 3        | 6        | 18       |
|          |          | Total    | **12**   |

Two passes over the same positions: multiply each pair, then add the products. That is the whole operation. The $\sum$ in the formula is just shorthand for "do this for every position and add".

The word that makes it useful is _agreement_. At each position, a large value paired with a large value gives a big positive product; a positive paired with a negative gives a negative product that pulls the total back down. So the single number coming out of a dot product is a score of how much the two vectors agree across their positions, and a score that can go negative when they disagree.

```python title="dot_product.py"
import numpy as np

a = np.array([1.0, 2.0, 3.0])
b = np.array([4.0, -5.0, 6.0])

print(np.dot(a, b))     # 4 - 10 + 18 = 12
print(a @ b)            # 12, same operation
print(np.sum(a * b))    # 12, element-wise multiply then reduce
```

The third line is the operation spelled out in NumPy: `a * b` produces the products column of the table, `.sum()` produces the total.

The same quantity has a geometric form:

$$
\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b} = \|\mathbf{a}\|_2 \|\mathbf{b}\|_2 \cos(\theta)
$$

Read that as: the length of $\mathbf{a}$, times the length of $\mathbf{b}$, times the <Tip term="cosine of the angle between them">For an angle theta: 1 when theta is 0 degrees, 0 at 90 degrees, -1 at 180 degrees. It turns an angle into a number in [-1, 1].</Tip>. $\theta$ is the angle between the two arrows, and $\|\mathbf{a}\|_2 \|\mathbf{b}\|_2$ is just the two lengths multiplied together.

The arithmetic version and the geometric version are the same number computed two ways, and that is what makes the dot product so useful: you never have to measure an angle, but the result behaves as if you did. The lengths are always positive, so the sign of a dot product comes entirely from `cos(theta)`, which gives the rules that make similarity work:

| `a . b`  | Angle       | Meaning                         |
| :------- | :---------- | :------------------------------ |
| Positive | Under 90°   | Pointing broadly the same way   |
| Zero     | Exactly 90° | Orthogonal, no shared direction |
| Negative | Over 90°    | Pointing broadly opposite ways  |

### A vector you can build yourself

The fastest way to make "entries of a vector" concrete is a <Tip term="term frequency vector">A document turned into a list of counts: one entry per word in the vocabulary, holding how many times that word appears in the document.</Tip>. A document becomes a vector where each number records how many times a particular word appears in it.

Suppose there are two documents:

- Document A: `apple apple banana`
- Document B: `apple banana banana`

First build a **vocabulary**: the list of all unique words across both documents, which here is `[apple, banana]`. That vocabulary defines the positions. Now each document is one number per vocabulary word, in that fixed order:

| Vector     | apple | banana |
| :--------- | :---- | :----- |
| Document A | 2     | 1      |
| Document B | 1     | 2      |

So `A = [2, 1]` and `B = [1, 2]`. The dot product is `2*1 + 1*2 = 4`, which is a score of how much the two documents use the same words with the same emphasis. Divide by the two lengths and cosine similarity gives `4 / (2.236 * 2.236) = 0.8`: very similar, because both lean on the same two words in roughly comparable amounts.

```python title="term_frequency.py"
vocab = ["apple", "banana"]

def tf(doc):
    return np.array([doc.count(word) for word in vocab], dtype=float)

A = tf(["apple", "apple", "banana"])     # [2. 1.]
B = tf(["apple", "banana", "banana"])    # [1. 2.]

print(A @ B)                             # 4.0
print((A / np.linalg.norm(A)).round(4))  # [0.8944 0.4472]
sim = (A / np.linalg.norm(A)) @ (B / np.linalg.norm(B))
print(sim.round(4))                      # 0.8
```

The third line is cosine similarity computed the way a vector database does it: normalize once at index time, then a plain dot product per query. That shortcut is the next section.

Two properties of this construction carry straight through to real embeddings:

- **The vocabulary is the dimension.** Two documents only have a dot product if they were built against the same vocabulary in the same order. `[2, 1]` against `[banana, apple]` is a different number, and silently wrong.
- **Most entries are zero.** A real corpus has tens of thousands of distinct words, and a single sentence uses a few dozen. A 100,000-word vocabulary gives a 100,000-dimensional vector that is almost entirely zeros. That is the shape every text model lives in, and it is why sparse storage and matrix multiplication, not loops, are the only workable way to compute with these things.

## Projection

The **projection** of `a` onto `b` is the component of `a` that lies along `b`. It answers: how much of `a` is explained by the direction of `b`? Picture a light shining perpendicular to `b`: the <Tip term="shadow">The projection of one vector onto a direction: the part of the first vector that lies along the second.</Tip> `a` casts on the line through `b` is the projection.

Two versions of that answer get used, and the difference is only whether you keep the direction:

The scalar projection (a scalar length along $\mathbf{b}$) is:

$$
\operatorname{comp}_{\mathbf{b}}(\mathbf{a}) = \frac{\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b}}{\|\mathbf{b}\|_2}
$$

The vector projection (an actual vector pointing along $\mathbf{b}$) is:

$$
\operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf{b}}(\mathbf{a}) = \left(\frac{\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b}}{\|\mathbf{b}\|_2^2}\right) \mathbf{b}
$$

Both start with $\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b}$, which measures the alignment. Dividing once by $\|\mathbf{b}\|_2$ removes $\mathbf{b}$'s length so the answer is a plain number of units. Dividing by $\|\mathbf{b}\|_2^2$ and then multiplying by the vector $\mathbf{b}$ re-attaches $\mathbf{b}$'s direction while cancelling its length, so the result is an arrow along $\mathbf{b}$.

```python title="projection.py"
a = np.array([3.0, 4.0])        # points up and to the right
b = np.array([1.0, 0.0])        # points straight right

scalar = (a @ b) / np.linalg.norm(b)
vector = ((a @ b) / np.linalg.norm(b) ** 2) * b

print(scalar)   # 3.0   - 3 of a's 5 units point along b
print(vector)   # [3. 0.]  - that same answer, written as an arrow on b's line
```

Subtract the shadow from the original and you get the part `b` cannot explain. That leftover is called the **residual**:

```python title="residual.py"
residual = a - ((a @ b) / np.linalg.norm(b) ** 2) * b
print(residual)              # [0. 4.]
print(residual @ b)          # 0.0  - orthogonal to b, exactly
```

Splitting a vector into "explained by `b`" plus "orthogonal to `b`" is the geometric content of least-squares fitting. The direction `b` gets all the credit it can claim, and the residual is whatever is genuinely left over.

Projections are the mechanism inside attention. A query vector is effectively projected against key vectors, and the dot product score measures how much of the query lies along each key.

## Cosine similarity

The dot product mixes direction with magnitude. A vector of length 1000 pointing roughly your way out-scores a vector of length 2 pointing exactly your way. **Cosine similarity** removes magnitude and keeps only the angle:

$$
\operatorname{sim}(\mathbf{a}, \mathbf{b}) = \cos(\theta) = \frac{\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b}}{\|\mathbf{a}\|_2 \|\mathbf{b}\|_2} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n a_i b_i}{\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^n a_i^2} \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^n b_i^2}}
$$

It is bounded in $[-1, 1]$:

| Value | Angle | Interpretation      |
| :---- | :---- | :------------------ |
| `1`   | 0°    | Identical direction |
| `0.7` | 45°   | Strongly related    |
| `0`   | 90°   | Unrelated           |
| `-1`  | 180°  | Exact opposite      |

```python title="cosine.py"
def cosine_similarity(a, b):
    return float(a @ b / (np.linalg.norm(a) * np.linalg.norm(b)))

query = np.array([0.9, 0.1, 0.0])
doc_a = np.array([18.0, 2.0, 0.0])    # same direction, huge magnitude
doc_b = np.array([0.5, 0.5, 0.5])

print(cosine_similarity(query, doc_a))   # 1.0
print(cosine_similarity(query, doc_b))   # 0.6376
```

A raw dot product would have ranked `doc_a` enormously higher purely on length. Cosine sees that `doc_a` is a perfect directional match and `doc_b` is not.

### Why unit normalization makes cosine free

If both vectors are normalized to unit length ($\|\mathbf{a}\|_2 = \|\mathbf{b}\|_2 = 1$), the denominator becomes 1 and disappears:

$$
\operatorname{sim}(\mathbf{a}, \mathbf{b}) = \mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b} \quad \text{when } \|\mathbf{a}\|_2 = \|\mathbf{b}\|_2 = 1
$$

This is why vector databases normalize embeddings at index time, meaning when the documents are first stored rather than when a query arrives. Every retrieval query then costs one dot product instead of a dot product plus two length computations, and the database can run the identical code path whether the collection was declared as cosine or as inner product.

```python title="normalized_index.py"
embeddings = np.random.randn(10000, 768)
embeddings /= np.linalg.norm(embeddings, axis=1, keepdims=True)

query = np.random.randn(768)
query /= np.linalg.norm(query)

scores = embeddings @ query           # one matrix-vector product
top_5 = np.argsort(scores)[-5:][::-1]
```

The division on the second line normalizes every row independently: `axis=1` walks across the 768 entries of each row, and `keepdims=True` leaves the result shaped `(10000, 1)` so it divides each row rather than the whole array. The broadcasting page covers that rule in full. The last line sorts the 10000 scores ascending, slices the final 5, and reverses them with `[::-1]` so the best match comes first.

<Callout type="info">
  Magnitude is not always noise. Some embedding models encode confidence or frequency in vector
  length, and normalizing discards it. Check the model card before assuming cosine is the intended
  metric. OpenAI and most general-purpose text embedding models expect cosine.
</Callout>

## Distance metrics compared

Cosine similarity measures angle. Distances measure separation. They are related but not interchangeable.

| Metric          | Formula                  | Range         | Magnitude sensitive |
| :-------------- | :----------------------- | :------------ | :------------------ |
| Cosine distance | `1 - cos_sim(a, b)`      | `[0, 2]`      | No                  |
| L2 (Euclidean)  | `sqrt(sum((ai - bi)^2))` | `[0, inf)`    | Yes                 |
| L1 (Manhattan)  | `sum(abs(ai - bi))`      | `[0, inf)`    | Yes                 |
| Dot product     | `sum(ai * bi)`           | `(-inf, inf)` | Yes                 |

Each formula starts the same way: `ai - bi` is the difference in coordinate `i`. L2 combines those differences by squaring, summing, and taking the root. L1 adds their absolute values instead, which is why it is called Manhattan: you travel along the axes in blocks, never diagonally. The last column says whether stretching one vector by 10 changes the score, which is the practical difference between the similarity measures.

For unit-normalized vectors, squared L2 distance and cosine similarity measure the same thing with the numbers rearranged. Expanding $\|\mathbf{a} - \mathbf{b}\|_2^2$ follows the algebraic identity:

$$
\|\mathbf{a} - \mathbf{b}\|_2^2 = \|\mathbf{a}\|_2^2 + \|\mathbf{b}\|_2^2 - 2(\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b}) = 1 + 1 - 2(\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b}) = 2 - 2(\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b})
$$

The first two terms are constants, so the only part that varies between pairs is `a . b`, and it carries a minus sign. A larger dot product means a smaller distance. The two numbers are not equal to each other, cosine 0.9 gives squared L2 of 0.2 and cosine 0.5 gives 1.0, but they move in lockstep, so ranking candidates by one ranks them identically by the other. That is the "rank identically" claim, and it is why HNSW and IVF indexes in Phase 9 can serve cosine queries using an L2 or inner-product kernel without changing results.

## L1 and L2 norms

A **norm** assigns a length to a vector. Any function that behaves like length does is a norm, and the two that matter in machine learning are:

$$
\text{L1 norm}\colon \quad \|\mathbf{v}\|_1 = \sum_{i=1}^n |v_i| = |v_1| + |v_2| + \dots + |v_n|
$$

$$
\text{L2 norm}\colon \quad \|\mathbf{v}\|_2 = \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^n v_i^2} = \sqrt{v_1^2 + v_2^2 + \dots + v_n^2}
$$

The `_1` and `_2` subscripts name which norm, nothing more.

```python title="norms.py"
v = np.array([3.0, -4.0])
print(np.linalg.norm(v, 1))    # 7.0   = |3| + |-4|
print(np.linalg.norm(v, 2))    # 5.0   = sqrt(3^2 + 4^2)
print(np.abs(v).sum())         # 7.0   L1 written out by hand
```

The second argument to `np.linalg.norm` picks the norm, and leaving it off gives L2.

The two norms disagree about what "big" means, and that disagreement is the entire story of regularization. `||[1, 1]||_1` is 2 while `||[1, 1]||_2` is 1.41. Spreading the same total weight across two coordinates costs 2 in L1 and only 1.41 in L2, whereas concentrating it in one coordinate as `[2, 0]` costs 2 in both. L2 punishes concentration, L1 does not.

Their unit circles have different shapes, and the shape explains everything about how they regularize.

Set a budget on the norm and ask which weight vectors are allowed to be used. The allowed set, `||w|| <= t`, is a **ball**: in two dimensions the L1 ball is a diamond with its corners on the axes, and the L2 ball is a circle. A "unit circle" is the edge of that region when the budget is 1.

<Mermaid
  chart={`

The other half of the picture is the loss. Every set of weights that produce the same loss form a ring around the best answer, and those rings are the **contours**. Training settles on the lowest contour that still reaches inside the allowed region, so the solution is wherever a contour first touches the ball's edge.

The L1 ball has sharp corners sitting on the coordinate axes, so the contour usually makes contact at a corner, and a corner has some coordinates equal to exactly zero. The L2 ball is smooth, so the optimum lands somewhere along a curve and shrinks every weight a little without zeroing any.

<Callout type="info">
  ### The takeaway in two lines

L1 regularization has diamond-shaped constraint regions in two dimensions, with corners on the axes, and the optimum often lands on one of those corners where one or more weights equal zero. L2 regularization has smooth, circular constraint regions, so the optimum is less likely to land exactly on an axis and the weights stay nonzero.

L1 can eliminate weights because its penalty has a sharp corner at zero. L2 generally only shrinks weights because its penalty is smooth and its gradient becomes zero at zero.

</Callout>

### Why norms become regularizers

Up to here the norms have only measured the size of a vector. They enter training when you start charging the model for using a big one:

- **L1 regularization (Lasso)** adds a penalty based on the absolute values of the model weights. It can push some weights exactly to zero, effectively removing the features those weights depended on.
- **L2 regularization (Ridge)** adds a penalty based on squared weights. It generally makes the weights smaller without forcing as many of them to become exactly zero.

### Lasso and Ridge

Lasso and Ridge are regularization techniques that prevent overfitting by penalizing large model weights. The main difference is that Lasso can make weights exactly zero while Ridge generally shrinks weights toward zero.

That geometry becomes those two regularization schemes. Adding a norm penalty to an unconstrained loss function $\mathcal{L}_0$:

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{Lasso}} = \mathcal{L}_0 + \lambda \|\mathbf{w}\|_1 = \mathcal{L}_0 + \lambda \sum_i |w_i|
$$

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{Ridge}} = \mathcal{L}_0 + \lambda \|\mathbf{w}\|_2^2 = \mathcal{L}_0 + \lambda \sum_i w_i^2
$$

Lasso and Ridge are the names from statistics for L1- and L2-penalized linear regression, and the names stuck. $\lambda$ is the penalty strength: raise it and weights shrink more; set it to zero and the penalty disappears.

<Callout type="info">
  ### Why charge for weight size at all

A model reaches very large weights by leaning hard on whichever features happened to be reliable in the training set, which is <Tip term="overfitting">Fitting the training data so closely that the model also memorizes its noise, so it performs worse on data it has not seen.</Tip>. A norm penalty trades a slightly worse training fit for smaller, more distributed weights that hold up outside the training set.

</Callout>

| Property          | L1 / Lasso                   | L2 / Ridge                    |
| :---------------- | :--------------------------- | :---------------------------- |
| Solution          | Sparse, exact zeros          | Dense, small values           |
| Feature selection | Built in                     | None                          |
| Gradient at zero  | Constant, does not vanish    | Proportional to `w`, vanishes |
| Solved in         | Linear models, sparse coding | Weight decay in deep learning |

"Sparse" means many weights are exactly 0.0, so those connections might as well not exist. "Dense" means all of them survive at small values.

The mechanism is visible in the penalty's own derivative, which is what pushes on a weight during training:

- L1 contributes `lambda * sign(w)`, where `sign(w)` is `+1` for a positive weight and `-1` for a negative one. The push has the same strength whether `w` is 3.0 or 0.0001, so it drives small weights all the way to zero.
- L2 contributes `2 * lambda * w`, which shrinks in step with `w` itself. As the weight approaches zero the push fades, and the weight parks at some small non-zero value.

<Callout type="warn">
  Modern LLM training uses **decoupled weight decay** in AdamW, which is L2-style shrinkage applied
  directly to the parameters rather than added to the loss. The distinction matters: adding it to
  the loss makes the penalty pass through Adam's per-parameter rescaling, which divides each
  gradient by an estimate of its own size, and that weakens the penalty unevenly across weights.
  Phase 3 covers the AdamW formulation.
</Callout>

## Interview questions

<Accordions>
  <Accordion title="Why is the dot product the right score for attention?">

    It answers whether two vectors are compatible, and it grows with both alignment and magnitude. A query and a key that point the same way score high, which is exactly the retrieval behaviour attention needs.

  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="What is the difference between cosine similarity and the dot product, and when are they the same?">

    Cosine is the dot product divided by the product of the two magnitudes, so it measures orientation alone. When both vectors are already unit-normalized the denominators are 1 and cosine equals the dot product. The main difference is: dot product considers both direction and magnitude, while cosine similarity considers only direction.

  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Why do vector databases index normalized embeddings with L2 distance?">

    For unit vectors, `(x - y) dot (x - y)` expands to `2 - 2 * (x dot y)`, so squared L2 is an affine function of cosine and ranks candidates identically. The index gets to use the distance metric it is built for while the ranking stays cosine.

  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Why does L1 produce sparse weights while L2 only shrinks them?">

    The L2 penalty pulls proportionally to the weight itself, so as a weight approaches zero the pull does too and it never lands exactly on zero. L1 pulls with the same force regardless of magnitude, so small weights get driven out all the way.

  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="What does projecting one vector onto another mean, and where does it appear in model architecture?">

    The projection matrix `np.outer(v, v) / (v dot v)` gives the component of a vector along a direction, which is a rank-1 operation. Restricting updates to a small subspace spanned by such directions is what makes a LoRA update cheap.

  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Two documents have cosine similarity 0.95. What do you know about the angle between them?">

    `arccos(0.95)` is about 18 degrees, and it stays 18 degrees however you rotate both vectors together. Cosine ignores the norms, so documents with very different term counts can still be judged highly similar.

  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="When is cosine similarity the wrong choice for term-frequency vectors?">

    When magnitude carries meaning. A document that mentions a topic 50 times and one that mentions it twice point in the same direction and score near 1.0, even though the first is far more about that topic.

  </Accordion>
</Accordions>
