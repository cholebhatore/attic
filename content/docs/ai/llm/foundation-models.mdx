---
title: Foundation Models
description: How pre-training replaced task-specific training, the adaptation ladder from prompting to continued pre-training, and open-weights versus closed-source trade-offs.
---

Before 2018 the workflow was one model per task. Sentiment analysis needed a trained classifier, translation needed a separate sequence-to-sequence model, question answering needed a third. Each one was trained from scratch on its own labelled dataset, and none of the work transferred.

A **foundation model** is a model trained on broad data at scale such that it can be adapted to a wide range of downstream tasks (Bommasani et al., arXiv:2108.07258). The definition has two load-bearing parts: the training data is broad rather than task-specific, and the same weights serve many tasks after adaptation.

---

## The three eras of AI engineering

| Era                      | What you train                    | What you need                                     | Iteration time | Example                         |
| :----------------------- | :-------------------------------- | :------------------------------------------------ | :------------- | :------------------------------ |
| **Task-specific**        | A whole model                     | Thousands of labelled examples                    | Days to weeks  | A CNN per image class           |
| **Pretrain + fine-tune** | A classifier head, or all weights | A pretrained checkpoint plus hundreds of examples | Hours          | BERT with a linear layer on top |
| **Prompt + adapt**       | Nothing, or a small adapter       | A prompt template and a few examples              | Minutes        | GPT-4 with a system prompt      |

The economic consequence is that model capability became a purchasable input. Instead of budgeting a training run, you budget an inference call.

<Callout type="info">
  ### Emergent abilities, and the argument against them

Wei et al. (arXiv:2206.07682) reported that some capabilities appear only above a scale threshold: multi-step arithmetic and instruction following are at zero for small models and nonzero for large ones, with no smooth improvement in between. Schaeffer et al. (arXiv:2304.15004) argued the effect is an artifact of the metric. Exact-match scoring is discontinuous, so a model whose underlying per-digit accuracy improves smoothly still looks like it jumps from 0% to 100% on a five-digit problem. Switching to a token-level or Brier score makes the same curves smooth. Both papers are worth knowing; interviewers like asking which one you believe.

</Callout>

## Generative AI

A **discriminative** model learns `p(label | input)`. A **generative** model learns `p(input)` or `p(output | input)` and can sample new data from it.

Autoregressive language models are generative in the conditional sense: they model `p(x_t | x_1 ... x_t-1)` and produce text by sampling one token at a time. That single objective turns out to be a universal interface, because any task can be written as text completion.

| Task            | Discriminative formulation       | Generative formulation                |
| :-------------- | :------------------------------- | :------------------------------------ |
| Classification  | `softmax(W h)` over N classes    | Complete the string `"The label is:"` |
| Translation     | Encoder-decoder with beam search | Complete `"English: ... French: ..."` |
| Summarization   | Not naturally expressible        | Complete `"Article: ... Summary:"`    |
| Code generation | Not naturally expressible        | Complete the docstring and signature  |

Other generative families exist and use different mechanisms: diffusion models denoise latents for images, and autoregressive codecs generate audio tokens. The transformer backbone is shared.

## The adaptation ladder

Ordered from cheapest and least capable to most expensive and most capable.

| Rung                      | Weights change? | Data needed           | Cost per attempt    | Fixes                              |
| :------------------------ | :-------------- | :-------------------- | :------------------ | :--------------------------------- |
| **Prompt engineering**    | No              | Zero                  | Cents               | Format, tone, simple reasoning     |
| **Few-shot in context**   | No              | A handful of examples | Cents               | Style, output schema               |
| **RAG**                   | No              | A retrieval corpus    | Moderate            | Fresh or private facts             |
| **LoRA fine-tune**        | Adapter only    | Hundreds to thousands | Hours on one GPU    | Behaviour, domain style, formats   |
| **Full fine-tune**        | Yes             | Tens of thousands     | Days on a cluster   | Deep behaviour change              |
| **Continued pretraining** | Yes             | Billions of tokens    | Weeks               | New language, new domain knowledge |
| **Pretraining**           | From scratch    | Trillions of tokens   | Millions of dollars | A new foundation model             |

The rule that decides which rung to use: RAG for knowledge that changes, fine-tuning for behaviour that must be consistent. Retrieval cannot teach a model to emit a strict JSON schema on every call, and fine-tuning cannot keep a product catalogue current.

## Open-weights versus closed-source

**Closed-source** models are accessed only through an API. The weights, and usually the architecture and training data, are not released. **Open-weights** models publish the checkpoint so you can download it, run it, and modify it.

Open-weights is not the same as open source. Almost no frontier model releases its training data or the full training pipeline. The OSI's open-source AI definition asks for the data, so it classifies Llama and Gemma releases as not open source even though the weights are downloadable.

| License family               | Examples                             | Restrictions worth reading                                                                                                                       |
| :--------------------------- | :----------------------------------- | :----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Apache 2.0 / MIT**         | Qwen 3, DeepSeek-V3, OLMo 2, Mixtral | None beyond attribution and patent grant                                                                                                         |
| **Custom community license** | Llama 4, Gemma                       | Use thresholds (Llama's clause triggers at 700 million monthly active users), naming requirements, no training competing models in Gemma's terms |
| **Proprietary API**          | GPT, Claude, Gemini                  | No weights. Rate limits, data retention terms, no self-hosting                                                                                   |

### The four trade-offs

**Cost.** An API charges per token with no fixed cost, so low traffic is cheap and high traffic is expensive. Self-hosting inverts that: the GPUs cost the same whether they are idle or saturated. The break-even point depends on your sustained token throughput, and it is usually reached sooner than people expect for high-volume workloads.

**Privacy.** API calls send your data to a third party. Self-hosting keeps it inside your network. This is the deciding factor for healthcare, legal, and any workload governed by data residency rules. Enterprise API tiers with zero-retention clauses narrow the gap but do not eliminate the transfer.

**Latency.** An API adds network round-trip time and queueing behind other tenants. A local model on dedicated hardware has neither, and you control the batch size. A local model on a single consumer GPU is usually slower than a well-provisioned API, because the API provider runs optimized kernels across many accelerators.

**Control.** Self-hosting gives you the logits, the sampling loop, the ability to quantize, the ability to edit weights, and the ability to pin a version forever. An API can change the underlying model under a stable alias, deprecate a version, or raise prices.

| Requirement                      | Choose             |
| :------------------------------- | :----------------- |
| Lowest time to production        | Closed API         |
| Data cannot leave the network    | Open weights       |
| Need logit-level access          | Open weights       |
| Spiky, low-volume traffic        | Closed API         |
| Sustained high volume            | Open weights       |
| Need the absolute best benchmark | Usually closed API |

## Where a model runs

<Mermaid
  chart={`

    A["Client<br/>browser or SDK"] --> B["API Gateway<br/>auth, rate limit, routing"]
    B --> C["Tokenizer"]
    C --> D["Scheduler<br/>continuous batching"]
    D --> E["GPU Pool<br/>tensor-parallel replicas"]
    E --> D
    D --> F["Sampler<br/>stop tokens, penalties"]
    F --> G["Streaming Response<br/>SSE chunks"]
    G --> A
`}
/>

Two properties of this pipeline matter for cost and latency.

**Prefill and decode are different workloads.** Prefill processes the whole prompt in one parallel forward pass and is compute-bound. Decode generates one token per forward pass and is memory-bandwidth-bound, because every step must read the entire weight matrix and the KV cache to produce a single token. That asymmetry is why output tokens are billed at a higher rate than input tokens, commonly several times higher, and why providers cache repeated prompt prefixes.

**Batching is where throughput comes from.** A single request underuses a GPU badly. Continuous batching admits new requests into a running batch as soon as a slot frees, rather than waiting for the whole batch to finish. It changes the scheduler's unit of work from a request to a decode step.

## Interview questions

<Accordions>
  <Accordion title="What is the definition of a foundation model, and which two properties distinguish it from a task-specific model?">

    Trained on broad data at scale and adaptable to a wide range of downstream tasks (Bommasani et al., arXiv:2108.07258). The two load-bearing parts: the training data is broad rather than task-specific, and one set of weights serves many tasks after adaptation.

  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Name the three eras of AI engineering and what changed economically at each transition.">

    Task-specific (train a whole model on thousands of labels, days to weeks), pretrain plus fine-tune (train a classifier head or all weights from a checkpoint, hundreds of examples, hours), prompt and adapt (train nothing or a small adapter, a template and a few examples, minutes). At each transition the cost moved from owning a training run to buying an inference call, and capability became a purchasable input.

  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="What is the difference between a discriminative and a generative model, and which one is an autoregressive LLM?">

    A discriminative model learns `p(label | input)` and only classifies. A generative model learns `p(input)` or `p(output | input)` and can sample new data. An autoregressive LLM is generative in the conditional sense: it models `p(x_t | x_1 ... x_t-1)` and emits one token at a time.

  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="When does RAG beat fine-tuning, and when is fine-tuning the only option?">

    RAG when the facts change or are private — catalogues, tickets, internal docs — because you re-index instead of retraining, and the answer stays citable. Fine-tuning when the requirement is behaviour rather than knowledge: a strict output schema, a fixed tool-call format, house style. Retrieval cannot make a model reliably emit valid JSON on every call.

  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title={'Why is "open weights" not the same as "open source"?'}>

    Open weights means the checkpoint is downloadable and modifiable. Open source, by the OSI definition, also requires the training data and the pipeline. Almost no frontier release publishes the data, so Llama and Gemma count as open weights but not open source.

  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Name two restrictions in a custom community license that an Apache 2.0 release does not have.">

    A usage cap: the Llama Community License required a commercial agreement once a product passed 700 million monthly active users. A no-competition clause: some community licenses forbid using model outputs to train another model. Apache 2.0 has neither, only attribution and a patent grant.

  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Give the cost, privacy, latency, and control arguments for self-hosting an open-weights model.">

    Cost: at steady high volume, per-token API pricing exceeds the amortized cost of the GPUs. Privacy: prompts and outputs never leave your own network, which is what a data-processing agreement cannot buy. Latency: no provider-side queue and no shared rate limit, and you choose the batching policy. Control: you own the weights, so quantization, sampling, fine-tuning and version pinning stay your decision instead of a deprecation email.

  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Why do API providers charge more per output token than per input token?">

    Input tokens are handled in one parallel prefill pass where the GPU is compute-bound and every weight read amortizes over hundreds of tokens. Output tokens come one per sequential decode step, and each step moves the whole weight set plus the KV cache to produce a single token. More GPU-seconds per token, so a higher price per token.

  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="What is the difference between prefill and decode in terms of what the GPU is bottlenecked on?">

    Prefill is compute-bound: tall, wide matrices across the whole prompt, and high GPU utilization. Decode is memory-bandwidth-bound: a single row multiplied against the same weights, so time is spent moving bytes, not doing arithmetic.

  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="What does continuous batching change about how a scheduler admits work?">

    A static batch admits a fixed set of requests and holds the slot until the longest one finishes. Continuous batching admits and retires requests at the level of a single decode step, so a finished sequence frees its slot and a waiting prompt enters on the next step.

  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Summarize the Wei et al. and Schaeffer et al. disagreement about emergent abilities.">

    Wei et al. (arXiv:2206.07682) measured abilities that sit at zero for small models and appear abruptly past a scale threshold, multi-step arithmetic among them. Schaeffer et al. (arXiv:2304.15004) showed the abruptness comes from the metric: exact-match scoring on a five-digit problem is a discontinuous function of a smoothly improving per-digit accuracy, and a token-level or Brier score turns the same curves smooth.

  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="A startup needs strict JSON output on every call and must not send patient data to a third party. Which rungs of the adaptation ladder are available to it?">

    Both constraints bite. No third party means a closed API is out, so an open-weights model self-hosted on your own hardware. Strict JSON on every call means prompting, few-shot and RAG are not sufficient on their own, so the ladder starts at LoRA — fine-tune a self-hosted checkpoint — with prompting and RAG alongside it for the facts.

  </Accordion>
</Accordions>
