---

Two questions sit behind every training run: how big should the model be for a given compute budget, and how do you spread it across devices. The first is a scaling law. The second is a parallelism strategy, and the answer depends on what is too large to fit.

---

## Kaplan's laws

Kaplan et al. (arXiv:2001.08361) fit power laws to training loss against parameters `N`, data `D`, and compute `C`:

```
L(N) = (N_c / N)^alpha_N        alpha_N = 0.076
L(D) = (D_c / D)^alpha_D        alpha_D = 0.095
L(C) = (C_c / C)^alpha_C        alpha_C = 0.050
```

Three claims from the paper:

1. Loss depends strongly on scale and weakly on architecture details like depth versus width.
2. Smooth power laws hold over seven orders of magnitude, with no visible plateau.
3. Large models are more sample-efficient, so for a fixed compute budget you should train a bigger model on less data.

Claim 3 gives the optimal split `N ∝ C^0.73` and `D ∝ C^0.27`. Compute goes mostly to parameters. GPT-3 followed it: 175B parameters on 300B tokens, a ratio of about 1.7 tokens per parameter.

---

## Chinchilla

Hoffmann et al. (arXiv:2203.15556) rederived the same relationship three independent ways and got a different answer. Their conclusion: `N_opt ∝ C^0.50` and `D_opt ∝ C^0.50`. Parameters and tokens should scale together, which gives the rule of thumb **20 training tokens per parameter**.

They trained Chinchilla, 70B parameters on 1.4 trillion tokens, and compared it to Gopher, 280B parameters on 300 billion tokens, at matched compute. Chinchilla reached 67.5% average accuracy on MMLU against Gopher's 60.0%, and cost roughly 7.5x less to serve.

Gopher's ratio was 1.07 tokens per parameter. By Chinchilla's law it was undertrained by a factor of nearly 20 in data.

The paper's fitted loss surface:

```
L(N, D) = E + A / N^alpha + B / D^beta
E = 1.69    A = 406.4    alpha = 0.34    B = 410.7    beta = 0.28
```

The `E` term is the irreducible entropy of the data. No amount of scale removes it, and it is why loss curves flatten rather than reaching zero.

---

## Why frontier models ignore Chinchilla

Chinchilla optimizes training compute. It says nothing about inference compute, and for a deployed model inference dominates total cost. A smaller model trained on far more data costs more to train and much less to serve, and the serving cost is paid every day forever.

| Model       | Params | Training tokens | Tokens per param | Note                         |
| :---------- | :----- | :-------------- | :--------------- | :--------------------------- |
| GPT-3       | 175B   | 300B            | 1.7              | Kaplan-optimal               |
| Gopher      | 280B   | 300B            | 1.1              | Severely undertrained        |
| Chinchilla  | 70B    | 1.4T            | 20               | The reference point          |
| Llama 1 65B | 65B    | 1.4T            | 21.5             | Chinchilla-optimal           |
| Llama 2 70B | 70B    | 2T              | 28.6             | Slightly overtrained         |
| DeepSeek-V3 | 671B   | 14.8T           | 22               | Near-optimal on total params |
| Llama 3 8B  | 8B     | 15T             | 1,875            | Deliberately overtrained     |

Llama 3 8B's ratio is not a mistake. The Llama 3 paper (arXiv:2407.21783) argues that a small model trained on a very large dataset performs comparably at inference to a much larger one, and that the inference saving outweighs the extra training cost by a wide margin. DeepSeek-V3's 671B is closer to Chinchilla because MoE lets it pay for 37B of active compute per token while holding 671B of capacity.

<Callout type="info">
  ### Compute accounting

The standard approximation is `C ≈ 6 N D` FLOPs: 2 per parameter for the forward pass, 4 for the backward. It ignores attention's `O(N^2)` term, which is why it underestimates long-context training. DeepSeek-V3's published budget was 2.788M H800 GPU hours on 2,048 GPUs, at a stated cost under $5.6M.

</Callout>

## What does not fit

Before choosing a strategy, identify the constraint. Memory per parameter during training:

| Component               | Bytes per param |
| :---------------------- | :-------------- |
| bf16 parameters         | 2               |
| bf16 gradients          | 2               |
| fp32 master copy (Adam) | 4               |
| fp32 momentum           | 4               |
| fp32 variance           | 4               |
| **Total**               | **16**          |

A 7.5B model needs 120 GB before a single activation is allocated. That is ZeRO's headline figure (Rajbhandari et al., arXiv:1910.02054), and it explains why optimizer state, not weights, is the first thing you shard.

### Data parallelism

Replicate the full model, split the batch, all-reduce the gradients. Communication volume per step is `2 * model_size` (reduce-scatter plus all-gather, equivalent to one all-reduce).

It does not help when the model itself does not fit. That is what ZeRO and FSDP fix, by sharding the redundant state instead of replicating it:

| Stage  | What is sharded  | Memory per GPU at 7.5B, 8 GPUs |
| :----- | :--------------- | :----------------------------- |
| ZeRO-0 | Nothing (DDP)    | 120 GB                         |
| ZeRO-1 | Optimizer states | 15 + 15 + 90/8 = 41 GB         |
| ZeRO-2 | Plus gradients   | 15 + 15/8 + 90/8 = 28 GB       |
| ZeRO-3 | Plus parameters  | 120/8 = 15 GB                  |

ZeRO-3 and PyTorch FSDP are the same idea. The cost is that ZeRO-3 needs an all-gather of parameters before every layer's forward and backward, so communication grows as memory shrinks.

### Tensor parallelism

Shard individual weight matrices. Megatron-LM (Shoeybi et al., arXiv:1909.08053) showed the pattern that makes it work with one all-reduce per block:

- The MLP's first Linear is **column-parallel**: each GPU holds `[d, d_ff/t]` and computes an independent slice of the hidden activation. No communication.
- The second Linear is **row-parallel**: each GPU holds `[d_ff/t, d]` and produces a partial sum. One all-reduce combines them.
- Attention shards by head, which is naturally parallel, then the output projection is row-parallel.

Result: two all-reduces per transformer layer in the forward pass, two more in the backward. That is heavy, so tensor parallelism degree is capped at the number of GPUs in one node, where NVLink bandwidth is an order of magnitude above the inter-node fabric.

### Pipeline parallelism

Shard by layer. GPU 0 holds layers 0 to 15, GPU 1 holds 16 to 31, and activations flow forward while gradients flow back.

The problem is the bubble: with `p` stages, stage `k` is idle until stage `k-1` has produced something. Splitting the batch into `m` microbatches interleaves the stages and shrinks the idle fraction to

```
bubble_fraction = (p - 1) / (m + p - 1)
```

With 4 stages and 32 microbatches that is 8.6%. GPipe (Huang et al., arXiv:1811.06965) runs all forward microbatches then all backward ones and needs to store every activation. PipeDream's 1F1B schedule interleaves them so peak activation memory is bounded by `p` microbatches instead of `m`.

### Sequence parallelism

Two distinct things share the name.

**Megatron-SP** (Korthikanti et al., arXiv:2205.05198) shards the parts tensor parallelism leaves replicated: LayerNorm, dropout, and the residual stream, split along the sequence dimension. It replaces one all-reduce with an all-gather plus a reduce-scatter, same volume, but it cuts activation memory by the TP degree.

**Ring Attention / DeepSpeed-Ulysses** (Liu et al., arXiv:2310.01486; Jacobs et al., arXiv:2309.14509) shard the sequence itself so a context longer than one GPU's memory can be trained. Each device holds a contiguous block of positions and passes its KV block around a ring, or exchanges heads via all-to-all, so every block eventually attends to every other.

### Expert parallelism

Shard the `E` experts of an MoE layer across devices. Each token must be dispatched to whichever GPU holds its selected expert and the result combined back, which is an all-to-all in both directions. The communication volume scales with tokens times `k` times `d_model`, and it is the reason MoE training needs a fast interconnect even when the per-token compute is small.

## Choosing

| Constraint                       | Strategy                    | Where it runs           |
| :------------------------------- | :-------------------------- | :---------------------- |
| Model fits, want more throughput | Data parallel / FSDP        | Anywhere                |
| Optimizer state does not fit     | ZeRO-1 through ZeRO-3       | Anywhere                |
| One layer's weights do not fit   | Tensor parallel             | Within a node only      |
| Layer count is too deep          | Pipeline parallel           | Across nodes            |
| Context does not fit             | Sequence / context parallel | Across nodes            |
| Expert count is large            | Expert parallel             | Within a node preferred |

Production runs combine all of them. A typical DeepSeek-V3-scale configuration puts tensor parallel within a node, pipeline parallel across nodes, data parallel across the whole cluster, and expert parallel orthogonal to pipeline. The degrees multiply to the total device count.

## Interview questions

<Accordions>
  <Accordion title="State Kaplan's optimal compute split and Chinchilla's, and explain why they disagree.">

    Kaplan: `N` goes as `C^0.73` and `D` as `C^0.27`, so compute mostly buys parameters because large models are more sample-efficient. Chinchilla: `N_opt` and `D_opt` both go as `C^0.50`, so parameters and tokens scale together. The disagreement comes from method. Kaplan fit loss against each axis while holding the others at correlated values, which biased the exponents; Hoffmann et al. rederived the split three independent ways (frontier fits, the parametric surface, a compute-free transfer method) and all three landed on equal scaling.

  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="How many training tokens per parameter does the Chinchilla rule of thumb specify, and what two numbers from the paper support it?">

    20 tokens per parameter. Chinchilla is 70B parameters on 1.4T tokens, exactly that ratio, and it beat Gopher, 280B on 300B tokens, at matched compute: 67.5% average MMLU against 60.0%, at roughly 7.5x lower serving cost. Gopher's 1.07 tokens per parameter was the counterexample.

  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title={<>What does the <code>E</code> term in the Chinchilla loss fit represent?</>}>

    The irreducible entropy of the data. It is the loss floor no model can predict past, which is why the surface saturates instead of heading to zero and why loss curves flatten at scale rather than continuing to fall.

  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Llama 3 8B was trained on 15T tokens. Compute its tokens-per-parameter ratio and explain why a lab would choose that.">

    15T / 8B = 1,875 tokens per parameter, about 94x the Chinchilla optimum. Chinchilla minimizes training compute and says nothing about inference compute, and a deployed model pays inference every day forever. A small model overtrained on enormous data costs more to build and much less to run, and the Llama 3 paper (arXiv:2407.21783) reports it matching a much larger model at inference.

  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Account for the 16 bytes per parameter in mixed-precision Adam training.">

    Two bytes of bf16 parameters, two bytes of bf16 gradients, and twelve bytes of Adam state in fp32: a 4-byte master copy, 4-byte momentum, 4-byte variance. That is why a 7.5B model needs 120 GB before a single activation is allocated, and why optimizer state is the first thing worth sharding.

  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="What does each ZeRO stage shard, and what does ZeRO-3 cost you in communication?">

    ZeRO-1 shards optimizer states, ZeRO-2 also shards gradients, ZeRO-3 also shards parameters. ZeRO-3 pays an all-gather of each layer's parameters before its forward and again before its backward, so communication grows as memory shrinks: 120 GB per GPU falls to 15 GB across 8 devices, and the extra traffic is the whole difference.

  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Explain the column-parallel then row-parallel pattern and why it needs only one all-reduce per MLP.">

    The first MLP Linear is column-parallel: each GPU holds `[d, d_ff/t]` and computes an independent slice of the hidden activation, so nothing needs exchanging. The second is row-parallel: each GPU holds `[d_ff/t, d]` and produces a partial sum of the full output vector, and one all-reduce adds those partials together. Attention mirrors it, shard by head then a row-parallel output projection. Two all-reduces per layer forward, two in backward.

  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Why is tensor parallelism degree limited to a single node?">

    Those all-reduces sit on the critical path twice per layer per step, so the degree is bounded by interconnect bandwidth. NVLink inside a node is about an order of magnitude above the inter-node fabric, and past the node boundary the communication cancels the compute saving that tensor parallelism was bought for.

  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Derive the pipeline bubble fraction for 4 stages and 32 microbatches.">

    `(p - 1) / (m + p - 1) = 3 / (32 + 3) = 3/35 = 8.6%`. Stage `k` cannot start until stage `k-1` produces something, and splitting the batch into 32 microbatches interleaves the stages so only the head and tail of the schedule sit idle.

  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="What does 1F1B change relative to GPipe?">

    GPipe runs every forward microbatch and then every backward one, which keeps activations stored for all `m` microbatches. 1F1B interleaves the two so each stage frees an activation as soon as its backward pass consumes it, bounding peak activation memory at about `p` microbatches instead of `m`.

  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Name the two different things called sequence parallelism and say what each one shards.">

    Megatron sequence parallelism shards what tensor parallelism leaves replicated: LayerNorm, dropout and the residual stream, along the sequence dimension, trading one all-reduce for an all-gather plus a reduce-scatter of the same volume to cut activation memory by the TP degree. Ring Attention and DeepSpeed-Ulysses shard the token sequence itself, each device owning a contiguous block of positions, so a context longer than one GPU's memory can be trained.

  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Why does expert parallelism require all-to-all rather than all-reduce?">

    Each token's destination depends on its own routing decision, so data moves from arbitrary sources to arbitrary destinations: a many-to-many dispatch and combine. All-reduce requires every device to hold an identically shaped shard and combine corresponding elements, which expert sharding breaks, and volume scales with tokens times `k` times `d_model`.

  </Accordion>
</Accordions>
